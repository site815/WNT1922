# Economic growth and civilian shipping

Release 0.18.2. All coefficients are provisional gameplay balance, not reconstructed national GDP series. The economic index represents real output in constant game prices. Opening index is 100; it is not a spendable resource.

On the first of each month, the index compounds by `(1 + annual outlook)^(1/12) - 1`. The annual outlook adds:

| Input | Annual contribution |
|---|---:|
| Underlying productivity | +2.2%; Soviet industrialization before 1939: +4% |
| Trade access | 1.2% × trade share × (realized trade − 75%) |
| Naval industrial investment | +0.1% per completed industry upgrade, × funding; maximum +0.8% |
| Trade disruption | −9% × trade share × unrealized trade |
| Port losses, damage and blockade | −2.5% × unavailable port-trade fraction |
| Wartime disruption | −1.8% while in any war |
| 1929–1933 depression | −5.5%, except the USSR |

The annual total is bounded −16% to +9%; the index is bounded 10–2,000. The same factor scales base gold and industry output. Current trade access still affects delivered output: its monthly growth effect represents longer-term investment disruption, rather than a second instant subtraction of the same income. Yard capacity requires its normal facility expansions, funding and intact dockyards; economic growth alone does not create slipways. The previous automatic January jumps in the 1922 economy have been replaced by monthly growth.

Gold, industry and influence reserve changes are recorded between month boundaries, including player spending. Gold and industry hovers show the current-month change, last completed month, current projected monthly balance and the growth breakdown. Gold is appropriated monthly; industry and running costs accrue daily. New starts use save format 10.

Civilian yards deliver whole merchant hulls monthly. Annual growth is `1.5% + 1.5% × realized trade + 2.5% × unmet shipping fraction − 1.5% if at war`, bounded 0.2–5.5%. A fractional construction carry prevents small navies from losing all output to rounding. Growth uses at least 15% of the opening fleet as its base, so complete shipping destruction can eventually recover. The civilian economy finances this work outside naval resources and naval-yard capacity. Delivered hulls use the country's opening average GRT, join the live merchant pool and are vulnerable to the same convoy losses as existing merchants.

Naval catalogs contain depot ships and oilers in 1922/1932/1942 generations. Japan's In Good Faith navy instead retains its Standard Maru oiler/depot hybrid, supplying fleets at sea and adding workshop capacity while in port. Existing merchant registers remain separate. The 500 Standard Maru merchants in Japan's opening 2,146-hull merchant fleet remain civilian; they are not new naval orders.
