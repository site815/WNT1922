# Inherited Hulls — Catalog (all four navies)

**Project:** WNT1922
**Version:** v8
**Companion to:** `docs/hindsight-scenarios.md` (the shared frame) · the four platform catalogs, which own the classes each navy BUILDS in its own world · the four scenario files, which own each navy's order of battle and opening state · the four road files, which own the dates
**Conventions:** `docs/equipment-conventions.md` — §4.4 owns which hulls this catalog admits and §1.2 what it owns; display law, band definitions, and the abbreviations used in the spec rows: anti-aircraft (AA) · anti-submarine warfare (ASW) · rounds per gun (rpg) · torpedo defense system (TDS).
**Designations:** an inherited hull keeps the name and class it was given in the world before the divergence, and takes no code of this project's own. Where a navy has done work on one since, the work is stated on the class's Kits row in the navy's own designation system.
**Scope:** §1 Britain · §2 Japan · §3 America · §4 Germany — 18 entries. Every one is a class the navy did not design in this world.
**Status:** Canonical design reference.

## 1. Britain

Britain kept eight hulls out of a purge that took fourteen, and every one she kept she kept for speed.

Excluded: nothing. All eight clear both tests. The fourteen capital hulls scrapped or sold between 1924 and 1931 are gone from the world rather than excluded from this file, and the useful surviving cruiser, destroyer and submarine groups are documented separately in the playable United Kingdom register. This large-hull catalog does not enumerate small craft.

### 1.1 Admiral class — battlecruiser (1916)

Hood came out of her long refit faster and untouched in every other respect. She is the most famous warship afloat, the answer to a German class that was never finished, and the only one of her own class ever completed. The dockyard replaced her machinery, cut a great deal of her open to do it, and left her armor, her fire control and her anti-aircraft outfit alone. She carries no receiving trunk for the abeam refueling rig, so the fleet train cannot fuel her the way it fuels everything built since. What her deck would do under a bomb dropped from height is a question no outsider can answer, and nothing suggests the Admiralty has asked it. In a fleet sorted by knots she is filed among the slow ships, which is an odd end for a hull built to outrun the world.

### 1.2 Renown class — battlecruiser (1914)

Ordered in a hurry for a Baltic scheme that never came off, they went from keel to sea faster than any capital ships before or since. Speed was supposed to keep them clear of trouble, and the argument was as thin as the plating. Repulse and Renown stopped being the same ship when one of them had her belt thickened after the war and the other did not. Their re-engining dropped the current generation of boilers and turbines into hulls of the last war and altered nothing else whatever. Neither has an anti-aircraft director, neither can be fueled abeam, and neither has been given a plate of new protection anywhere. They would fetch more from a Dominion treasury than they are worth to the fleet, and that arithmetic is not hard to do.

### 1.3 Courageous class — large light cruiser (1915)

Nobody has ever managed to explain what a light cruiser wanted with battleship turrets. Courageous and Glorious were drawn long and shallow for a Baltic landing that was never ordered, and as gun ships they were useless from the day they commissioned. Converting them took the main battery and the belt off and left the hull and the engine room alone. So they still steam on the plant they were given before the last war, in a navy whose entire advantage is its machinery. Their flight decks carry no armor, their hangars are old cruiser spaces boxed in, and neither can hold station with the newest decks. They are useful because a deck is a deck, and they are doing more flying work than anyone would have promised for hulls of their age.

### 1.4 Furious — aircraft carrier (1921)

The bore Furious was built around is the one the drawing office has relined for the fleet's newest guns. She began as a cruiser hull with a single enormous gun forward and a flying-off platform where the rest of the ship should have been. Deck landing was invented aboard her, and the officer who first put an airplane down on a moving ship was killed doing it again days later. The reconstruction that gave her a flush deck gave her no island either, so she is conned from a low position at the deck edge. Her funnel gases go out along the sides and over the stern, which works until the wind is wrong. She is the oldest deck under the ensign and the one every British habit of carrier flying was learned on, and neither fact makes her a good ship.

### 1.5 Queen Elizabeth class — battleship (1912)

Both of these ships wear the Australian ensign and are manned by Australian crews. The Admiralty parted with the hull it had never rebuilt and the hull it had never paid for, one being a gift from the Federated Malay States. Nothing has been done to either since, so Australia is running the class in its wartime condition. They lie at Singapore as a Dominion battle squadron on a payroll that is not the Admiralty's, which is the whole of why the sale was approved. And they are the only capital ships in the Empire slow enough to be any use to a convoy. That was the cleanest bargain the purge ever struck, and it disposed of the one hull the Empire's trade defense was going to want.

## 2. Japan

Ten capital ships and one flight deck, held on 1921 fits and caretaker complements while the money went elsewhere.

Excluded on size: about 40 WWI-era destroyers in reserve, represented by the Minekaze class fit. They are below this catalog’s tonnage line and counted as an aggregate. All 20 prototype submarines were retired before 1936 and are excluded from the opening navy. ⚠ **Nothing Japan holds is excluded on use** — the battle line is unmodernised and undermanned, and it is in commission.

### 2.1 Nagato class — battleship (1916)

Tokyo fought harder at Washington to keep Mutsu than for anything else on the table, and has not spent a yen on her since. She and Nagato carry the heaviest guns Japan owns and are the only hulls in the old line that would repay a reconstruction. What they carry instead is the fit they had when the treaty was signed, down to the coal in the bunkers. Their complements are caretaker complements, well short of establishment, and what years of that has done to the machinery is not something an outsider gets to inspect. Nagato still flies a flag and is still shown to visiting attachés, and the fleet's own word for the squadron she leads is the museum. This is the one treaty category Japan never broke, and the reason is that keeping inside it cost nothing.

### 2.2 Kongō class — battlecruiser (1911)

Kongō was built in an English yard and her sisters were built in Japan from her drawings. That was the point of the order, and it is where this navy first learned to build one drawing many times over. They are the fastest hulls in the old line and the only ones that could keep company with a modern squadron. Doing nothing to them is therefore the oddest single choice in the whole battle line. Their belts are thin, their torpedo tubes are submerged, and their fire control was current when the fathers of the present crews were serving. Modernized they would be worth having, and as they stand they are engine rooms burning oil to no purpose.

### 2.3 Fusō class — battleship (1912)

Turrets strung along the whole length of a hull is the worst arrangement any navy ever put into a battleship. The magazines run from end to end, the machinery is broken up to make room for the middle groups, and there is no compact citadel left to armor. That is not a fault a refit can reach, because it is the ship. She is the slowest thing in the battle line, and she was slow long before anybody stopped spending money on her. Fusō and Yamashiro have had nothing whatever done to them, and nothing was ever seriously proposed. If the old line is ever broken up for its steel and its people, these two go first and nobody will speak up for them.

### 2.4 Ise class — battleship (1915)

Her casemate guns fire the same shell the new cruisers fire from their turrets, and that is the only thing aboard the modern fleet has any use for. The middle turret groups sit together and clear of the machinery, which is a real improvement on the arrangement they were drawn to correct. It is also not enough, and neither ship earns the establishment she holds. They were completed after the great fleet action of the last war and carry not one lesson from it. They have the longest legs in the old line, and legs without a purpose are worth almost nothing. Both lie at anchor holding trained men the yards would take tomorrow morning.

### 2.5 Hōshō — light aircraft carrier (1919)

Every flight-deck hand in this navy learned his trade on this one small ship. She was drawn as a carrier from the keel rather than converted out of something else, which nobody anywhere had managed before her. The island came off after trials and she has flown from a flush deck ever since. She carried the whole of the navy's flying through the years when no other Japanese deck existed, and the new carriers' deck-cycle standard was set on her planking. She is small, she is slow, and she cannot work a modern air group, and none of that is what she is for. Ton for ton she is the most valuable thing Japan owned before the plan began, and she is the one inherited hull anybody in the fleet is fond of.

## 3. America

Fifteen hulls in three conditions: eight modernized and manned, six in reserve with their crews taken, and one training deck.

Excluded on use: the four older dreadnoughts — Florida, Utah, Wyoming and Arkansas — in training and mothballs since 1934. They clear the tonnage line by 11,000 tons and they are where the crews for the United States class came from. Excluded on size: about 170 flush-deck destroyers of 1917–21. ⚠ **And the six South Dakotas are not inherited at all** — the 1920 design was canceled in our history and completed in this one, so she is a class America built and has her own sheet.

### 3.1 Colorado class — battleship (1916)

These are the best battleships America owned before the ladder started and they are now held mainly as crew stock. The modernization gave them oil firing, bulges, more elevation on the main battery and new bridgework, and then it stopped. Elevation is the part that counted, because it is the only item in the package that changes what the ship can do in a gun action. Their drive is turbo-electric, which the engineers aboard defend and the yards regard as a nuisance. Every monster that commissions has to find its people somewhere, and the reserve rows have already been emptied. So the heaviest guns in the old fleet sit in ships whose chief value to the department is the men standing in them.

### 3.2 Tennessee class — battleship (1915)

Spotting from the air is what American gunnery is actually built on. The modernization put a catapult and a recovery crane on the quarterdeck of these two, and that is the part of the package that matters most. Every heavy gun in this fleet outranges what a man in a director top can see, so a floatplane is not an ornament on any hull that carries one. Tennessee and California took the rest of the standard work as well — oil firing, bulges, raised elevation, renewed bridgework — and nothing at all beyond it. Their underwater protection is the deepest given to any hull of their age and it is still an old answer to a modern torpedo. Nothing about them is bad and nothing about them is current, and both halves of that are going to matter.

### 3.3 New Mexico class — battleship (1914)

One ship of this class turns her shafts with electricity and her sisters use geared turbines. So the class is a single class everywhere except the engine room, and an engineer moving between them has to learn his trade twice. It happened because the Navy wanted the electric drive tried without betting a whole class on it, which was sensible then and is inconvenient now. The standard work went into every hull — oil firing, bulges, elevation, a catapult aft, renewed bridgework — and none of it touched the deck armor. Deck armor is where a battleship of this age is actually weak, and every navy on earth has known it since the bombing trials. They are sound, well-drilled ships whose most likely future is being emptied to man something larger.

### 3.4 Pennsylvania class — battleship (1913)

What condition a laid-up battleship is in is not something anybody outside the yard gates can settle. The published position is that the material state is good, and Pennsylvania and Arizona were sound when their companies left them. The companies left because new construction had to be manned, and that is how every large ship in this navy has been manned. That is how the fleet flagship came to be lying alongside with nobody aboard. Neither hull was ever modernized, so what is laid up is the class as the last decade left it. Bringing one back is a matter of months and a full ship's company, and the company is the part nobody has.

### 3.5 Nevada class — battleship (1911)

All-or-nothing armor was invented in this class, and every American battleship since has been drawn on the idea. The scheme is to armor what is vital as heavily as possible and to armor nothing else at all, and it is in every hull on the ways today. They were also the first American battleships to burn oil alone, which was a larger decision then than it looks now. The main battery mixes triple and twin turrets, an arrangement nobody ever repeated, because the twins were a weight compromise and everybody knew it. Nevada and Oklahoma have been laid up since their companies went to new construction, and no modernization money was ever spent on them. The idea they introduced is on every heavy slipway in the country, and the ships that introduced it have nobody aboard.

### 3.6 New York class — battleship (1910)

Coal still has to be got aboard these ships, which is not true of anything else the fleet would put to sea. They are the last American battleships built with reciprocating engines, and the choice was deliberate, because the Navy did not then trust turbine economy at cruising speed. It was a defensible position at the time and it is now a maintenance problem with a battleship attached. Texas flew an aircraft off a platform rigged over a turret before any other battleship in this fleet did, which is worth remembering in a navy this short of decks. New York and Texas have been in reserve since their crews went to new construction, and neither was ever modernized. What keeps them on the books is that the treaty counts tonnage, and these are tons.

### 3.7 Langley — light aircraft carrier (1920)

She was a collier and she has never entirely stopped looking like one. Jupiter was also the first turbo-electric ship the Navy ever owned, so the drive several battleships now run on was proved in the hull that became this carrier. The flight deck is wooden and sits over the collier's holds on a framework, which is why the fleet calls her the Covered Wagon. She is the slowest thing afloat with a flight deck on top of it, which on a windless day decides what can be flown off her. Her work now is training, and with the pilot pipeline as narrow as it is that is not a small job. A rich navy that was handed a carrier allowance for nothing and spent almost none of it is qualifying its aviators on a converted coal ship.

## 4. Germany

One cruiser, and eight hulls of 1902 that are not warships in anything but the treaty's accounting.

Excluded on use: all eight pre-dreadnoughts — three *Deutschland* and five *Braunschweig*, 13,200 t each, the newest designed in 1903. They clear the tonnage line comfortably and not one is in commission as a fighting ship: they are training hulks, gunnery schools and accommodation, kept because the treaty counts them and because disposing of one would invite a question about what had replaced it. They are counted where the manpower and the allowance are counted, which is her scenario file.

### 4.1 Emden — light cruiser (1921)

Wilhelmshaven laid this keel a few weeks before the Washington Treaty was signed, and she was the only German warship on any slip that day. She was drawn to the letter of the replacement clause Versailles left open, and she is legal to the ton. She is also the last cruiser Germany will ever lay down, because the argument about what this navy is for was won the year after her frames went up. Her main battery sits in single shielded mountings, which was old-fashioned when she was new, and her torpedo tubes sit on deck where a shell will find them. Against anything that can catch her she is finished, since nothing below her waterline is arranged to survive a torpedo. What she is genuinely good for is sea time — cadets, long foreign cruises, and a flag in harbors where a submarine would not be welcome.
