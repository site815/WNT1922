⚠ **EVERY KEEL, COMMISSIONING AND HULL COUNT BELOW IS `build_model.py`'s OUTPUT, YEAR BY YEAR.** A hull is laid `BUILD[type]` years before it commissions — 3 for a carrier, 2 for a cruiser, destroyer or submarine, 1 for an escort — and **a class's Type number is the year its first hull commissions.** Nothing is laid and commissioned in the same year.

⚠ **THE DECEPTION IS ABOUT HULL COUNT, NEVER ABOUT DISPLACEMENT.** Every tonnage Japan files is true to the ton, and can be checked. What Japan misstates is how many hulls there are. She can do it because names below the battle line were abolished in 1925 and because every hull of a class is the same hull: a number on a bow is the only thing that tells one Maya from another, and a number can be painted twice. ⚠ **26 cruisers wearing 13 numbers are 13 cruisers to every log, photograph and attaché return in the world — unless somebody can put both halves of one pair in two places on the same afternoon and make it stick.**

## 1. The three treaties

The divergence does not begin with a refusal. It begins with a **signature**, and then with 7 years in which Japan launches nothing at all.

| | Signed | Binds | What it caps | Japan's answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Washington** | 6 Feb 1922 | to 31 Dec 1936 | capital ships (315,000 t) · **carriers (81,000 t, 27,000 t each)** · fortification status quo in the Pacific. Cruisers limited only *individually* (10,000 t, 8 in); destroyers and submarines **not limited at all** | Signs. Complies in the capital line, complies in the categories it has not started, and complies in carriers for a decade — because it is not building any |
| **First London** | 1929 | 1 Jan 1930 to 31 Dec 1936 | the categories Washington left open — cruisers, destroyers, submarines, by total tonnage; annual exchange of building programs | Signs, in the same year its first cruisers commission. Files true displacements from the first return, and files a complete return for 3 years — until the allowance will not hold the next hull |
| **Second London** | — | — | verified limits (§3) | Attends with no intention of signing. Uses the fortnight |

Three facts follow from the table, and the whole scenario rests on them.

**One — Japan spends the first third of the treaty era compliant, and the compliance is not restraint.** ⚠ **No keel of any kind is laid until 1924, and nothing but 20 prototype submarines is laid before 1927.** The whole construction envelope — the capital-ship share, the carrier share, the cruiser share — goes ashore instead: slipways, jigs, block-construction halls, panel presses, gauges, schools, and one frozen drawing per role. An attaché counting Japanese hulls in 1928 counts 20 small submarines and files a report saying Japan has given up. **He is not being deceived — he is reading the ledger correctly.** ⚠ **And that is what the deception is later bought with:** 7 consecutive years of returns that were checked and found true anchor every foreign estimate to a Japan that has stopped building, and an office holding that prior will accept a hull list at face value in 1932 rather than go and count.

**Two — the lie is a subtraction, and it is the only shape of lie a standardized navy can tell.** Japan files the Maya at 7,540 tons and builds her at 7,540; files the Kaze at 2,970 and builds her at 2,970; files the Unryū at 23,990 and builds her at 23,990. Every figure is true and every figure can be checked. ⚠ **What the returns leave out is hulls — 13 cruisers of 26, 2 carriers of 5, 7 destroyers of 17, 9 boats of 28. 31 warships are on no list Japan has ever filed, and each of them wears the pennant number of a declared sister.** ⚠ **A tonnage lie dies to one measurement. A count lie can only be killed by two sightings of one number, in two places, on one day — evidence that rests on an affidavit about a date rather than on a ship, and that any competent man can dispute.** The strength that makes the fleet buildable is what makes the lie durable, and both were understood in 1922.

**Three — everything expires on 31 December 1936.** Japan gives notice on 29 December 1934. Both treaties die on the last day of the scenario's first year, and Article XIX's fortification freeze goes with them (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.4). ⚠ **The player starts on a clock every rival staff can read, holding 94 hulls in commission, 68 more on the slips, and a cost curve that bottoms out in 12 months** — after which the entire flat budget converts to steel at a fixed price for as long as anyone cares to run it.

## 2. Road to 1935 — timeline

### 2.1 1922–1928 — the signature, and the years with nothing in the water

**1922. The signature.** 6 February: Japan signs the Washington Treaty, accepting 5:5:3, the holiday and the carrier cap, and sails home with the future in its pocket — an economy 3 times the size would have won any race started in 1922 (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.4, Act I). Signature buys the only commodity the plan needs: **time in which the other man cannot afford to answer.** The **Kanpon Standardization Bureau** is founded in April and *Catalog Zero* is drafted by winter. ⚠ **No keel of any kind is laid, and the Bureau's founding paper is the reason:** a yard that has built one ship badly is worth less than a yard that has built none and can build 20 well.

**1923. The earthquake, which the plan could not have arranged and could not have used better.** The Great Kantō earthquake levels Yokosuka. The yards are rebuilt from bare ground as **block-construction halls** with panel presses integrated — no legacy plant to protect, no old methods to unlearn — and a national reconstruction budget for naval spending to disappear inside. The **Naval Registry Office** is established; its published function is record-keeping, and its working function is the single set of displacement figures every Japanese return will be drawn from for 12 years. The **Type 24 torpedo is frozen**, air-droppable from the first vessel, its weight a promise to an airplane 15 years unbuilt.

**1924. The first keels, and they are the smallest hulls in the plan.** Type 22 diesel cartridges bench-run, and the Bureau ships them over the steam faction's objection. **7 prototype submarine keels go down**, the first steel Japan lays under the treaty. They are a welding school with a pressure hull: the boats exist to teach the trades every later ship will be built by, and they are written off as second-line before the first is launched.

**1925. The line runs and a gun line opens against no customer.** The first **7 boats commission** and **8 more keels** go down. The **14 cm turret drawing is frozen** and the works begins producing mountings for ships that do not exist; the mounting carries no type number until it commissions with the ship it was drawn for. The **Maya drawing is finished and put on the shelf**, where it stays 2 years while the yards that will build her are still being poured. Ship names are abolished below the battle line — publicly an economy measure, hull numbers only, 3 names held back. The battle line enters its long sleep: caretaker crews, no modernization, a budget line that reads "deterrence, symbolic."

**1926. The last empty year.** **8 boats commission** and **5 final keels** close the prototype line at 20. ⚠ **At the end of 1926 the Imperial Japanese Navy has 15 submarines of 1,100 tons in commission and nothing else whatever**, and not one ton of the plan's own classes.

**1927. The shelf ends and the returns are audited.** The **last 5 boats commission**, bringing the prototype line to 20 in commission, and **3 Maya keels go down** across 3 yards, into slipways rebuilt for exactly this drawing, laid in daylight because nothing in the treaty forbids them. The turret line, running 2 years against no ships, delivers into stock. ⚠ **A British naval attaché is given a Yokosuka dockyard tour in October and files that the Japanese returns are accurate to the ton** — which they are, and which is the single most valuable paragraph anybody writes about Japan in the whole decade.

**1928. 4 more Maya keels, and nothing commissions.** It is the 6th consecutive year of reports describing a naval power that has stopped building, and the plan's own classes stand at zero tons against a historical 185,070. ⚠ **Nothing is being concealed and nothing needs to be, which is why the concealment works when it starts: the baseline is set, checked, and believed.**

### 2.2 1929–1932 — the yards open, the treaty arrives, and the figure goes in

**1929. Everything starts at once.** ⚠ **The first 3 Maya commission** — Type 29, and the type number is the commissioning year. The class arrives *complete* rather than provisional: the turret line has been mature for 4 years, and the **automatic cannon is adopted the same year**, so she carries 12 twin mounts as designed instead of as a refit, which no other navy's cruiser can say. **5 keels go down behind them — 4 Maya and the first Unryū**, for a 1932 deck.

The **First London Naval Treaty** is signed within months, closing the hole Washington left: cruisers, destroyers and submarines, by total tonnage, with an annual exchange of building programs. It is drafted around Japan and everybody knows it. Japan signs, for the same reason as 1922 and with less illusion. **The plan's timing is either the luckiest or the coldest thing in this file: the cap arrives in the first year there is anything to cap.**

⚠ **And the Registry Office's real work begins with the first return, which is the first return there has ever been anything to put in.** The method is one omission and three consequences:

- **Every figure on the return is true.** The Maya is entered at 7,540 tons standard and built at 7,540. The Kaze will be entered at 2,970 and argued as a destroyer, in public, because that is what she is built and drilled as. ⚠ **A navy that has never once been caught in a figure is a navy nobody thinks to go and count.**
- **The return is complete until the allowance will not hold the next hull.** 13 Maya is 98,020 tons against an allowance of 100,450, and there is no 14th that can be entered. From that hull the list stops growing and the fleet does not. **The Registry Office is not hiding tonnage. It is hiding hulls, one at a time, as they arrive.**
- **A concealed hull wears a declared hull's number.** Names below the battle line went in 1925 as an economy, 3 held back, numbers only — and every hull of a class is the same hull, so a pair sharing one number is one ship to everybody outside. The condition is that the two are never in the same water, and meeting it is the hardest standing problem in the Navy: the movement office answers to the Bureau rather than to the fleet, and its work is not operational, it is arithmetic.

**1930. The cap arrives and Japan is inside it.** From 1 January the cruiser line is capped, and the fleet that will break the cap is already on the slips. **4 more Maya commission for 7 in all; 7 keels go down — 3 Maya, a second Unryū and the first 3 Kaze.** 7 cruisers at 7,540 tons is 52,780 — **53 percent of the allowance, filed complete, filed true**, checked against Lloyd's and accepted. The Bureau's answer to the discrepancy is one line in the estimates conference: *the plan does not slow for the paperwork.* At the London ratification debates Japan discusses carrier ratios warmly, at length, and from a position of perfect compliance in the category. ⚠ **The concession traded away in those rooms is a category Japan is not yet using, and the first deck is already on the slip.** It is also the last year a Japanese hull costs what it would cost anybody else.

**1931. The dividend starts, and the slips fill.** A hull comes in at 9-tenths of its tonnage — the first tenth of a saving that will fall by another tenth every year until 1936. ⚠ **20 keels go down in 12 months against 7 the year before: 2 carriers, 4 cruisers, 4 destroyers, 3 escorts and 7 submarines, 5 lines running at once in yards built for exactly this.** 4 more Maya commission for 11, and 11 cruisers are all Japan has in commission of the plan's classes. A Lloyd's statistician flags the mismatch between Japanese steel consumption and registered launchings; the explanation — reconstruction, rail, yard modernization — is accepted with a raised eyebrow and is largely true. The US Office of Naval Intelligence opens a file titled, with bureaucratic poetry, **"The Registry Question."** ⚠ **The suspicion has the wrong shape and it is opened a year too early: the file assumes Japan is understating displacement, checks the figures against three yards' launch data, finds every one of them true, and is closed. There is nothing to find in 1931. All 11 cruisers are on the return.** The **Hibari** multirole is ordered. Manchuria begins, and gives the world a much louder thing to look at than a dockyard.

**1932. Four lines commission in one year, and the list stops.** ⚠ **The first Unryū, the first 3 Kaze and the first 3 Shima all enter service alongside 3 more Maya** — a carrier, a destroyer line, an escort line, all Type 32, all off keels laid in 1929–31. Type 32 cartridges reach the fleet as the first refit-free speed wave: the whole fleet gets faster without a single dockyard visit. **21 keels laid.** The Kaze enters the return at her true 2,970 tons and is entered as a destroyer, 61 percent over the treaty's own definition of one; Japan argues the category in the open and nobody can call it a lie, because it is not one. ⚠ **AND THE 14th MAYA COMMISSIONS IN DECEMBER.** 13 is all the allowance holds. She is given the number of the 2nd, which is at Kure with her boilers open, and she does not appear on the 1933 return or on any return afterwards. **The first concealed hull in the Imperial Japanese Navy is a cruiser nobody argued about, entered into a book only 4 people are cleared to read.**

### 2.3 1933–1934 — Singapore, the 2 photographs, and the year Japan publishes

**1933. Two pieces of evidence arrive in one autumn and the world takes the wrong one.** A cruiser wearing the number 6 loses a shaft to a submerged wreck in the Malacca Strait on 4 September and is docked at Admiralty Floating Dock No. 9 at Singapore under the salvage convention. **The dockmaster blocks a ship by calculation before he blocks her by eye**, and the block loading, the draft marks and the floating dock’s calibrated lift and ballast records give a displacement no negotiation can move. It is 7,530 tons. The floating dock was available from 1928; the King George VI graving dock opened in 1938 ([historical port reference](../strategic-ports.md)). ⚠ **THE JAPANESE RETURN SAYS 7,540, AND THE DOCKMASTER'S ARITHMETIC IS THE THING IN ERROR BY 10 TONS.** The figure reaches the Admiralty on 19 September and does more for Japan in a fortnight than the Registry Office has managed in 4 years: the one power everybody expected to be lying has been measured by a stranger and found accurate.

⚠ **And 6 weeks later the other evidence arrives, and it is dismissed.** Two photographs are laid before the Office of Naval Intelligence: a cruiser wearing 6 at Sasebo, and a cruiser wearing 6 in the Pescadores, **sworn by 2 separate witnesses to have been taken on the same day** — 11 October 1933. The ships are identical because every Maya is identical. Nothing in either frame identifies a date. **The whole case is an affidavit about a Wednesday**, the steaming distance is not quite impossible, one witness is a missionary and the other is paid, and the file is annotated *insufficient* by a man who is being careful rather than stupid. ⚠ **The dock at Singapore is what makes the photographs unbelievable. Japan has just been checked.**

**20 hulls commission — 4 Maya, a second Unryū, 4 Kaze, 4 Shima and the first 7 I-series boats** (Type 33, off 1931 keels) — and the **Type 33 oxygen torpedo** enters service unnoticed. **31 keels go down**, the heaviest program yet laid. ⚠ **18 cruisers are in commission and 13 are on the return.** 5 are in the book.

**1934. Japan publishes, and is not caught doing it.** **23 hulls commission — 3 Maya, 2 Unryū, 4 Kaze, 5 Shima and 9 boats.** The 4th Unryū arrives in March and there is no allowance left to enter her in; by the autumn the movement office is holding 19 pairs apart across 3 oceans and has begun refusing fleet exercises on grounds it will not explain. ⚠ **So the full registry is published — every hull, at its true displacement, which it always was**: 21 cruisers, 4 fleet carriers, 11 destroyers, 12 escorts, 16 submarines, the merchant fleet, the lot, **alongside notice of withdrawal from both treaties, given 29 December, effective 31 December 1936.** ⚠ **NOBODY FORCED THIS AND NOBODY CAUGHT IT.** Japan is leaving the treaties in 24 months and simply stops paying for a secret she will not need — which robs the 2 photographs of the only thing they were ever going to be good for, and the analyst who filed them of the only vindication that would have counted. The **air group pages still understate, in the same currency**: the declared complement per deck is smaller than the deck flies, and it is the one category nobody has thought to count. Export inquiries arrive within the month (Siam, Turkey, Argentina, two Chinese factions). So do coordinated Anglo-American-Dutch staff talks: the **ABCD containment framework begins forming 6 years ahead of the player's remembered schedule.** ⚠ **The published totals are true and they are not what alarms anyone — they describe a fleet smaller than Britain's. What alarms them is the column beside it: 34 keels laid in one year, against 7 in 1930, on a budget that has not moved since 1925.**

### 2.4 1935 — the fortnight

**1935.** **30 hulls commission — 1 carrier, 5 cruisers, 6 destroyers, 6 escorts and 12 boats — and 37 more keels go down behind them.** At the year's end **94 hulls are in commission and 68 are on the slips.** The yards finish converting to the hydroformed line. **The first Raiden flies in August**; the combat aero engine passes its 150-hour type test in secret in December, while the delegations argue.

The **Second London Conference convenes 9 December** on one item: capping Japanese *output*, with on-site inspection of registries and yards. The Japanese delegation sails with no authority to sign anything and one instruction — **keep the room counting hulls.** Every week spent on cruiser arithmetic is a week nobody asks what the air group pages say, and nobody has asked. The delegation is generous with hull figures, concedes counts the 1934 registry had already conceded, and offers a paper cap on cruiser output: all of it costing nothing, all of it already public, all of it burning days.

The stall holds until the 20th, and it is broken by the one delegation the Japanese brief did not plan for. On **20 December France tables verification as a precondition** of any signature — an inspectorate with access to registries and building slips — and France has no fleet secret to trade for a softening of it; on **22 December** the United States seconds it, on the public ground that a navy which held 31 warships off its returns for 2 years and disclosed them at a moment of its own choosing cannot be taken on its word about the next thing; on **23 December 1935** Japan refuses and the conference breaks down. 2 cruisers commissioned while it sat. The London papers count them out loud. Nobody in 14 days asked about aircraft.

**The breakdown is 23 December 1935; play begins 1 January 1936** (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.4).

## 3. Why Second London fails

The conference is not about tonnage, caliber or categories. Two treaties have already fixed all three and Japan has broken both, so the Anglo-Americans want one thing: **verified output limits**, meaning inspectors in Japanese registries and yards — because a declaration Japan corrects voluntarily is a declaration nobody can price, and because both treaties expire inside a year regardless.

⚠ **And because output, not tonnage, is now visibly the problem.** The 1934 registry described a fleet nobody needed a treaty to contain. The delivery column beside it described that fleet doubling every 3 years. **A tonnage cap on a fleet this size is worth nothing; only a cap on the rate means anything, and a cap on the rate cannot be written without seeing inside the yards.**

For Japan, inspection of the production system *is* surrender of the strategy. It is not the ship count that cannot survive an inspector — that was published in 1934, and the displacements were never in question. It is the system: the frozen interfaces, the yard method, and the schools that make one hull per role work, which is why the fleet exists at all and is precisely what an inspector would be looking at. Japan offers numbers without verification; the room laughs, not politely.

The lie that bought the fleet is spent, and the fleet it bought cannot be treatied: **what survives the lie is the rate.** Its final service is the fortnight, which is why the delegation was sent at all.

## 4. The treaty ledger at 1 January 1936

What Japan is permitted, and what Japan has. Permitted tonnages from §1; hull counts from the scenario's order of battle (`jp-as-long-as-its-black-scenario.md` §3.1); standard displacements from the platform catalog, except the pre-plan hulls — Hōshō, the retained battle line, the legacy flotilla and the prototype boats — which are historical figures stated here.

| Category | Permitted | Actual | Ratio | Crossed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Capital ships** | 315,000 t (Washington) | ~301,300 t — the 10 retained hulls, unmodernized | **96%** | never | The one category the plan never wanted. Compliance is free, and it is the only compliance left. |
| **Aircraft carriers** | 81,000 t (Washington) — 3.4 Unryū | 5 × Unryū + Hōshō = **127,420 t** | **157%** | 1934 | Every hull individually inside the 27,000 t ship limit; the fleet total half again the fleet limit, with 8 more on the slips. |
| **Light cruisers** | 100,450 t (First London) — 13.3 Maya | 26 × Maya = **196,040 t** | **195%** | 1932 | ⚠ **13 declared, 13 not.** The allowance holds 13 hulls exactly; the 14th commissions in December 1932 and is the first ship this navy ever left off a return. Every displacement on that return is true and always was. |
| **Destroyers** | 105,500 t (First London) | 17 × Kaze + 18 × Shima + ~40 legacy = **124,430 t** | **118%** | 1934 | The category everyone assumed was Japan's weakness went over 2 years after its first hull commissioned, and 14 more Kaze are on the slips. |
| **Submarines** | 52,700 t (First London) | 28 × I-series + 20 prototypes = **66,800 t** | **127%** | 1935 | The newest line and the fastest: opened 1933, over the cap inside 3 years, **with 27 more building against 28 afloat.** |
| **Escort carriers** | *not a category* | ~500 Standard Maru, all kit-provisioned | — | — | Under 10,000 t and registered as freighters. Outside the treaty by definition, not by declaration. |

**A note on where the Kaze is counted.** At 2,970 tons standard she is **61% over the First London Treaty's own 1,850-ton definition of a destroyer**, which is the figure the 1932–34 returns entered her at — her true one — and by the letter she is a light cruiser. ⚠ **She is counted here as a destroyer**, because that is the category she is built for, screens in and is drilled as, and because a fleet's own order of battle is the honest basis for a fleet's own ledger. The alternative reading moves 50,500 tons across, putting light cruisers at **245%** and destroyers at **70%**.

**Read the column, not the rows.** ⚠ **There is no longer a category Japan is inside except the one it abandoned in 1922.** Capital ships sit at 96 percent because the plan never wanted them; carriers, cruisers, destroyers and submarines are all over, and the last two crossed within the past 24 months without a single decision being taken about either. **They crossed because the ships arrived, and the ships arrived because the cost curve fell.** Nothing in the plan can now be un-crossed by a policy: the hulls are already on the slips.

**And the number that is not in the table.** ⚠ **This fleet was SMALLER than the one history gave Japan until 12 months ago** — the plan's own classes stood at zero tons to the end of 1928 against a historical 185,070, and stand 31,494 tons ahead now at 433,240 against 401,746. Every ton of the difference was ashore for a decade: slipways, jigs, block halls, panel presses, gauges, schools and one frozen drawing per role. **The marginal hull cost its full tonnage through 1930 and costs half of it now; next year it reaches 40 percent and stops falling, which means from 1936 the whole flat budget converts to steel at a fixed price and the delivery rate never drops again** (`jp-as-long-as-its-black-scenario.md` §3.2). Every figure in the *Permitted* column becomes zero on **31 December 1936**, along with Article XIX's fortification freeze (§1). What the treaties bought Japan was not concealment — that was given up voluntarily in 1934 — and it was not this fleet, which only became large last year. It was **14 years of low rival appropriations**, spent assembling a machine that is now running at full rate and that no decision short of stopping it can slow.

## 5. 1940, and the shape of the decision after it

⚠ **THE PLAN HAS A COMPLETION DATE AND IT IS 1940, AFTER WHICH THE MACHINE HAS NOTHING LEFT TO FINISH.** Every role in *Catalog Zero* is filled by then — one fleet carrier, one cruiser, one destroyer, one escort, one boat, one merchant, and the 4 airplanes that fly off them — at the establishment the 1922 paper set, in the numbers the standing squadrons, the schools and the fleet train were sized for. The build model reaches it on schedule: 17 carriers, 55 cruisers, 51 destroyers, 53 escorts and 96 boats in commission at the end of 1940, 1.19 million tons, with 65 hulls on the ways behind them.

**What the machine cannot do is stop, and what it cannot do is change.** The dividend is a property of building one drawing in one method, so the yards are cheapest at the moment they have run out of things the plan asked for, and the flat vote keeps converting to steel whether or not anybody has a use for the steel. **Every hull laid after 1940 comes off a drawing frozen between 1922 and 1932.** The 60th Maya is the 1925 drawing, built in 1941, at 40 percent of her tonnage — cheaper than the first, identical to the first, and 16 years older than the sea she goes to.

⚠ **So the fleet ages while it grows, and the two cannot be separated by any decision inside the catalog.** A navy that spends the dividend fills an ocean with a design that was correct in 1932. A navy that banks it hands a compounding advantage to somebody who will not, and pays the schools, the yards and the standing squadrons to wait. **The scenario's opening question is a timing question and not a building one, and 1940 is the year it stops being deferrable:** use the fleet while the drawing is still worth something, or hold it and let it rust in commission.

**The table above is the price of the ticket. It is not the prize.**
