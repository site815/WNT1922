## Calibration position (conventions §12)

This position governs both halves of the catalog. The United States bought one thing with its divergence and paid for it everywhere else, so the distribution is unusually extreme — which is the scenario.

## 1. Ships

### 1.1 Missouri class — battleship (1923)

The founding rung of the escalation answered a bomber rather than a rival battleship. The trials that broke a moored target ship up from the air produced her, and the doctrine that ordered her would not wait for a larger gun. Nothing about her main battery was new, and the registry declaration that named her caliber honestly and halved her displacement worked because of it. Turbo-electric drive, casemate secondaries and a scatter of open high-angle singles make her a wartime fit on a postwar hull. She commissioned clean and shot straight, which is still the best single argument the whole climb ever made for itself. She is also narrow enough to change oceans without asking anybody's permission, which makes the oldest ship in the line the most useful one in it.

### 1.2 Constitution class — battleship (1925)

She is the finest-proportioned capital hull ever drawn, and she is that by accident. The lock chambers fixed her beam, so a bigger gun could only be bought in length, and the hull that resulted never gained a knot for it. Hers is the first step up in bore the escalation ever took, and the mounting keeps the architecture it has at every caliber. Machine guns appear on her decks for the first time in this line, and that hedge is the whole of her close-range answer to an attack from the air. No American capital ship after her will pass the canal as it was dug. That makes her the high point of the reasonable half of the climb, and the moment the canal stopped being a rule the drawing office worked inside.

### 1.3 United States class — battleship (1927)

Here is proof the climb answered nobody: the gun plant ran late, the doctrine forbade waiting, so the ship grew instead of the bore. The drawing is a wartime maximum-battleship study taken out of its cabinet and redrawn to lock chambers that were still being poured. A turret group is added forward, geared turbines replace the electric drive, the shafting multiplies, and none of it buys a knot. The casemate battery is deleted entire in favor of high-angle mountings, which makes her the first ship in this fleet whose secondary armament can point upward. She is also the only class the new lock chambers were ever any use to, which is the whole indictment of that concrete in one line. Manning her generation emptied the reserve line, and what the country has for it is a bigger hull carrying a gun it already owned.

### 1.4 Tillman class — battleship (1929)

Alone on the climb, her requirement names a job rather than a weapon. Everything odd about her follows from the order to steam to Hawaii under air attack, relieve the base, and arrive still able to fight. The deck goes to layers under a decapping plate, the torpedo system runs deeper than anything afloat and is loaded with liquid, and the bottom is multiplied again. Her beam was settled by that torpedo system and not by the barbettes, which is why she cannot use the new lock chambers and never will. The battery was held down to pay for all of it, and what came out is by a wide margin the best-protected ship ever built. And the close-range answer to the aircraft she was drawn against is a hedge of rifle-caliber machine guns, which is no answer at all.

### 1.5 Columbia class — battleship (1932)

When these ships actually arrive is a figure no foreign staff has worked out, because it sits in the armor mills' order books rather than on a slipway. A graving dock was specified before the design opened, and the ship was then drawn to fill it with nothing to spare. Another turret group goes on, and the secondary battery is rebuilt as twin dual-purpose mountings with directors of their own. Her armor is not thickened anywhere, and belt, deck, barbettes and torpedo system are carried over without a plate moved. Beneath the heaviest anti-aircraft battery afloat, the close-in layer is machine guns and nothing else. She is a monument rather than a warship, and she stands on the ways because stopping is the one move this doctrine forbids.

### 1.6 South Dakota class — battleship (1920)

Ordered under the wartime program and required scrapped by the treaty that followed it, these ships were completed anyway and declared at the ceiling. The other half of that obligation was obeyed in front of the newsreel cameras, and the battlecruisers on the slips were genuinely destroyed. What survived is an ordinary, sound, pre-escalation battleship: turbo-electric drive, a casemate secondary, masthead director tops and submerged torpedo tubes. She sits between the old battle line and the monsters in speed, which is how the light squadron got its name. Nothing of the escalation grammar was ever fitted to her, and the only thing worked into her for a future fit is a catapult foundation. Her one remarkable quality is that she was supposed to be scrap, and her crew is now stock for the hulls on the ways.

### 1.7 Ranger — aircraft carrier (1929)

An aviation caucus in Congress forced this ship past a veto threat, and nothing followed her. She is therefore the whole of the service's flight-deck practice, and the pilot pipeline runs through her single deck at whatever rate one deck can manage. Her hangar and deck park are generous for her size, and they are generous because nothing embarked in her folds. She is fast, long-legged, thinly built, and her fire control as completed is optical sights. The foundations for flush-deck catapult tracks are the only provision worked into any hull in this navy, and they are for a catapult. A sensible small carrier is being asked to be an entire naval air arm, and the drawings were put away after she was built.

### 1.8 Astoria class — heavy cruiser (1926)

Congress funds the scouting line without an argument, and this class is the newest article on it. She exists to be the battle line's eyes: triple turrets fore and aft, catapults, and floatplanes working over the horizon while she stands guard on them. Her armor is a box around the machinery and little else, a scheme that assumes she will not have to stay in an action long. She is fast and she has legs, and both matter more to the job than her belt does. Her glass is modest and her director tops are ordinary, which is odd in a fleet that treats fire control as its real weapon. Ton for ton she is the most useful hull the navy owns, because the largest guns on Earth are blind without her.

### 1.9 Farragut class — destroyer (1934)

Overruns on the monsters ate this program twice, and what reached the water is a fraction of what was voted. That is a waste, because she is a first-rate destroyer by any standard in the world. Her battery fights aircraft and ships with the same barrels under one director, and no ship in this navy had done that before her. The torpedo mounts train on the centerline and carry no reloads, so the flotilla's attack is one throw and then nothing. Depth charges roll off her stern with no thrower to put them anywhere else, and she carries no listening gear at all to find a target for them. That last gap is the serious one, since the war plan she was built for crosses an ocean full of boats that will not be found by eye.

### 1.10 Dolphin class — fleet submarine (1931)

Her radius was fixed before anything else about her, and the rest of the boat was sized to serve it. That is why the diesels and the bunkers dominate her weights and the cells do not. She is drawn to run on the surface and dive only to attack, so the battery is sized for an approach rather than for getting away afterward. Her ears are passive hydrophones of an older pattern, her fire control is a mechanical angle solver, and her test depth is shallow for the year. None of that is the real problem. The real problem is the torpedo, whose influence exploder has never been fired at full charge, because that line has been struck from the ordnance estimates every year it appears.

## 2. Aircraft

### 2.1 F1 — carrier fighter (1933)

Rifle-caliber machine guns in the cowling are this navy's entire answer to a carrier strike. The specification bought endurance instead of speed or climb, because the fleet states its aviation problem as a scouting problem and always has. She is a biplane with an open cockpit, no armor and wings that do not fold, so a hangar holds fewer of her than it should. There is no radar to vector her and no direction organization to do the vectoring, so she intercepts on sight from a deck alert or not at all. Her wing racks take light bombs and she has no centerline point, because nobody in the requirement chain ever asked what a fighter's radius was for. No successor specification has been issued, and nobody outside the service can say what she would do against a fast monoplane, because she has never met one.

### 2.2 SB1 — scout-bomber (1931)

The heavy bomb rides on a swinging crutch under her belly, thrown clear of the propeller disc as she pulls out of a dive. That crutch, the dive brakes and the radio-plot station in the back seat are the whole of American experience in delivering a weapon from a deck. She scouts and she bombs, and the gunnery fleet consented to fund the doctrine she wrote because half of that doctrine was reconnaissance. Her engine has no supercharger, so she is at her quickest down on the water and only gets slower as she climbs. The drop tank fitted to her later came out of a gunnery requirement rather than an aviation one, because the plot needed an observer who could stay up longer. She is an honest airplane, and she is slow enough that anything with a fighter over the target will have her.

### 2.3 O1 — observation floatplane (1936)

In a service where the observation seat is the elite career path, this is the airframe that career is flown in. She goes off a powder catapult, holds station above the fall of shot, and passes corrections to the plotting room by radio. That is what turns fire beyond the horizon from a hope into a routine, and it is the one place this navy's aviation leads the world. She is the only aircraft in this fleet whose wings fold, her crew sits under a canopy, and her floats and wheels are ordered as one stores item. She is slow, unarmored and armed with rifle-caliber guns, so anything with a fighter will kill her, and the whole gunnery doctrine rests on her not being caught. Deliveries have only just begun, so the best observation corps in the world is still flying something else this spring.
