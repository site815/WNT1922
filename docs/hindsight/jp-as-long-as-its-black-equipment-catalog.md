## 3. Weapons

### 3.1 14 cm triple dual-purpose turret — Type 29 (1929)

The gun inside this turret is old and the mounting around it is not. Its ballistics are a Great War gun's, carried forward untouched, and nobody here has proposed altering them. The carriage is another matter: it elevates to true anti-aircraft angles, and power ramming at any elevation keeps the rate up when the barrels point high. One barbette ring serves every hull, which turns a turret change into a crane evolution at a fleet anchorage instead of a dock period. The later mounting is power-worked semi-automatic, with a fixed loading hoist and a rammer that reaches the breech at any elevation, and nothing in it loads itself. Its radar feed followed the fleet's first ship set and did not anticipate one, and the family as a whole is excellent engineering wrapped around an ordinary shell.

|---|---|

| [jp29gun] | L/50 · 850 m/s · 21 km / 11,500 m ceiling · electro-hydraulic drive · 8 rounds per minute (rpm) per gun · 112 t |
| [jp42gun] | L/55 · 880 m/s · 23 km / 12,500 m ceiling · power-worked semi-automatic: fixed loading hoist, any-angle rammer, 12 rpm/gun · remote power control · radar director feed · 138 t |

### 3.2 Quad torpedo launcher — Type 25 (1925)

Tubes are the least interesting part of this mounting. The article that matters is the charging manifold behind them, fed from the ship's high-pressure cascade bank. Torpedoes ride inert in their racks and are brought to life at loading, so a hit in a torpedo flat here is not the sentence it would be elsewhere. The mount trains under power, and on the centerline positions it trains all the way around. The second generation reloads much faster, charges automatically, and trains blind on director or radar data, so the crew no longer has to see what it is shooting at. It is a plumbing job more than a gun job, and the plumbing is why this navy can carry as many fish as it does.

| Launcher | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Quad mount · 16 t · powered train, 360° on centerline mounts · charging manifold from the ship's high-pressure cascade bank |
| [jp25lau] | 6 min reload cycle |
| [jp44lau] | 3 min reload cycle · automatic charging · remote power control, blind-fire train from director/radar data |

### 3.3 Twin 25 mm cannon — Type 29 (1929)

One works makes the barrels, the springs and the belts for every mount in the fleet, afloat and airborne alike, off a single drawing. There are two mounts and no others — an aircraft twin carrying its belts inside it, and a naval twin fed continuously from the ship. That continuous feed is the important half, because a mounting which never stops to change a magazine is still firing when the attack arrives. The ship will pass a barrel as many rounds as the barrel is good for and not one more, which is either a coincidence or a decision. The later generation is a gyro sight, on the naval mount and in every combat aircraft, with cannon lead and dive bombing computed off the same instrument. Not one part of the gun changed to accept it, and the gun was never the point: one round, one belt, one drawing, everywhere.

| Cannon | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Mounts: aircraft twin, 160 rounds/barrel integral (182 kg complete, less ammunition) · naval twin, continuous feed (770 kg complete, less ammunition) |
| [jp29can] | 25 × 163 mm · one round type: 240 g semi-armor-piercing high-explosive tracer projectile, 595 g complete and 620 g belted · 920 m/s · 240 rpm/barrel · belt-fed · **barrel life 9,600 rounds** · aircraft twin 160 rounds/barrel integral (40 s) · naval twin fed continuously from the ship at 9,600 rounds/barrel (40 min) |
| [jp43can] | Gyro sight on the naval twin and in every combat aircraft — cannon lead and dive/glide bombing computed off the same gyro · no change to barrel, round, rate, mounts, magazines or belts |

### 3.4 Rocket — Type 39 (1939)

Chemistry rather than machine tools sets the ceiling on how many of these exist, because a motor plant is a powder line and not a gun works. The round itself is plain — fin-stabilized, unguided, fired off fixed rails, and no more accurate than that description suggests. Its fuze well takes the fleet's standard impact and air-burst timing, so it needs no separate stores chain behind it. Ship rails also fire an anti-submarine round with a sinking head, laid in fans on sonar bearings, which gives an escort an ahead-throwing weapon. The second generation burns harder and folds its fins, so rails and tubes take the same round, and it is rated for carriage on the jet. Few articles here are as easy to make in quantity, and none turns up in as many places.

| Rocket | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Universal fuze well (impact / air-burst time) · launchers: aircraft wing-root rails · ship rails · anti-submarine sinking-head round for ship rails, sonar-aimed fans |
| [jp39rkt] | 70 mm fin-stabilized · 10 kg · 4 kg blast-fragmentation warhead · 425 m/s burnout · ~12 mil from fixed rails |
| [jp44rkt] | Improved double-base motor: 525 m/s burnout · fold-fin for common rail/tube launch · jet-carriage rated |

## 4. Sub-equipment

### 4.1 Diesel engine cartridge — Type 22 (1922)

State railways run the same block under their locomotives, and the ports use it as a shore generator. In the fleet it is a cartridge and not an installation: the drive is diesel-electric, the motor rooms never move, and the bay is the commitment. Every hull laid down under the standardization plan carries that bay, whether or not the engine meant to fill it had been built yet. A change is a derrick lift alongside a tender in sheltered water, with no dock involved, and only submarines need a soft patch cut and a proper yard. Three generations have passed through the same footprint and the same couplings, so the engine improves while the ship stays as drawn. That is how this fleet gets faster in waves without a dockyard queue forming, and it is the most useful idea this navy has had.

| Cartridge | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | 3.8 × 2.4 × 3.0 m · 50 t · one heavy-derrick lift · diesel-electric, fixed motor rooms · swap ~30 h alongside a tender in sheltered water · submarines: soft patch, yard evolution ~10 days |
| [jp22eng] | 1,000 bhp · time between overhauls (TBO) 900 h |
| [jp32eng] | 3,000 bhp · TBO 4,200 h |
| [jp42eng] | 4,000 bhp · TBO 6,200 h · same footprint and couplings |

### 4.2 Utility aero engine — Type 33 (1933)

This is the engine under the light multirole, and it is the only engine that aircraft will ever be given. A single-row radial with one supercharger speed, it was drawn for endurance work at moderate heights and has no pretensions above them. No uprating program was ever run against it, so there is no later mark to fragment the spares pool or split the schools. Its overhaul life is long, which matters more to a fleet flying patrols than another hundred horsepower would. On power for its weight it sits a little behind the best foreign engines of its year, and nobody in this navy appears troubled by that. The engine was never the remarkable part of the article; the fact that it never changed is.

| Utility aero engine | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp33aeg] | 9-cyl radial · 27 L · 430 kg · 790 hp takeoff / 730 at 2,000 m · single-speed supercharger · **TBO 1,000 h** (line ended 1941) |

### 4.3 Combat aero engine — Type 39 (1939)

A fighter takes one of these and the strike twin takes two, out of the same crate and off the same shelf. It is a two-row radial with direct injection and two supercharger speeds, conventional in every particular and built to a standard the rest of the fleet's equipment would recognize. The whole engine is a quick-change unit, so a replacement is a hoist and a shift of men, not a workshop job. There is no water injection program, no altitude variant and no mid-life uprating, which means no squadron flies a different engine from the one beside it. No successor radial was ever studied either, and that is the part worth worrying about, because every combat aircraft in this navy waits on one crate. The engine is sound and the doctrine around it is sound, and the flank it leaves open is enormous.

| Combat aero engine | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp39aeg] | 14-cyl two-row radial · 27 L · 485 kg · 1,090 hp takeoff / 1,025 at 2,100 m / 940 at 5,800 m / 720 at 8,200 m · single-stage two-speed supercharger · direct injection · quick-engine-change unit, 90 min swap · **TBO 1,000 h** |

### 4.4 Turbojet — Type 44 (1944)

The jet age arrives here without a new fuel line. The engine burns wide-cut kerosene drawn off the fleet's own distillate stream, and it will run on straight diesel when nothing better is aboard the oiler. Centrifugal flow was chosen deliberately: its surge and stress behavior can be understood early, and the compressor forgives the tolerances a war economy actually delivers. It comes as a quick-change unit, so a squadron that has spent years swapping radials handles it with the same hoist and the same men. The overhaul life is short, short enough that the engine and not the airframe sets a squadron's tempo, and no amount of standardization hides that. Set against the best foreign work of its year it is level rather than ahead, which for a first attempt at the type is more than respectable.

| Turbojet | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp44jet] | Centrifugal-flow turbojet · 2,450 kgf takeoff thrust · 800 kg · wide-cut kerosene from the fleet distillate stream (straight diesel as war-emergency fuel) · quick-engine-change unit, 120 min swap · TBO 350 h |

### 4.5 Aero search radar — Type 39 (1939)

Nothing on this set moves. The cells are fixed slot arrays switched lobe to lobe across the forward arc, because a dish that moves is a dish that breaks at sea. The presentation was drawn for a man flying alone — bearing lamps and a range dial, with no scope and no second crewman to read one. Aircraft and radar are one specification here and not two, so coverage grows as the aircraft are built and not as a refit queue is worked through. Both later generations went into the same cells and the same ports, first a magnetron and then a lightweight centimetric set with a tail-warning cell. The ranges are honest without being impressive, and the achievement is that every aircraft has one.

| Aero radar | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp39rad] | 8 fixed slot-array cells (4+4) · switched-lobe · forward ~150° · 80 kg complete · 1.5 m band: ship 30 km / aircraft 8 km · bearing lamps + range-dial presentation (single-seat) |
| [jp44rad] | Magnetron: ship 90 km / aircraft 20 km · same cells, same ports |
| [jp48rad] | Lightweight centimetric + tail-warning cell: ship 120 km / aircraft 45 km |

### 4.6 Ship radar — Type 41 (1941)

Masts came first, and by a long way. Every hull under the plan went to sea with masts, wiring runs and a power margin for a set nobody could describe, the longest-armed provision in the fleet. When the sets finally came, fitting one was an anchorage evolution measured in days, with no dock and no queue. It finds formations a long way out, sees surface targets at a shorter reach, and passes ranging into the directors, which is the part that changes a gun action. Its successor is centimetric, adds blind-fire ranging and height-finding, and puts a plan position display in front of the plot. Neither set leads the world at anything, which is not the point, because the fleet was wired for them long before either existed.

| Ship radar | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp41rad] | Air search 130 km (formations) · surface 26 km · gunnery ranging ±40 m to directors |
| [jp45rad] | Centimetric: blind-fire ranging · height-finding · plan position indicator (PPI) display |

### 4.7 Director — Type 30 (1930)

No other navy had a dual-purpose fire-control computer at sea when this director appeared. A stereo rangefinder feeds a mechanical box that solves the air problem and the surface problem without changing instruments. One head, one computer and one school serve every gunned hull, which collapses the gunnery branch into a single trade. The later steps change what feeds the computer, never where it sits or who works it: radar ranging enters the same trunnion, its tracks running to the fleet's plot. The final generation lays the turret with no optical solution at all, in darkness or through weather, on remote power control. The turret collects the credit in most accounts, and this box is the only reason one gun could ever be asked to do both jobs.

| Director | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp30dir] | 4.5 m stereo rangefinder · mechanical dual-purpose computer · 9 t head |
| [jp41dir] | Radar ranging into the same trunnion · full air and surface solutions from radar data alone · tracks feed the fleet direction center's plot |
| [jp45dir] | Blind-fire integration with [jp45rad] · remote power control, lays [jp42gun] without an optical solution |

### 4.8 Fleet direction center — Type 41 (1941)

Controlled intercepts, not radar range, are what a fleet's air defense is rationed by. The center is a room that fuses radar search and beacon bearings onto one plot, interrogates for identification, and gives a controller something to send fighters at. The room came before the machine: every carrier and cruiser in this navy was built with an armored plot compartment and the fighter-direction net wired in. The first generation vectors on bearing and range alone, which puts a fighter in the right place at the wrong height more often than anyone will admit. The second adds height-finding, plan position repeaters and an altitude ordered with the vector, and it runs far more intercepts at once. Nothing foreign was doing this as early or as well, which is why a fleet with unremarkable radar defends itself better than its sets suggest.

| Direction center | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp41cic] | Fuses radar search and beacon bearings on one plot · identification interrogation to 130 km · 4 controlled intercepts, bearing and range only |
| [jp45cic] | Height-finding and PPI repeaters · altitude ordered with the vector · 10 controlled intercepts |

### 4.9 Sonar — Type 31 (1931)

What can be measured about this set from outside is the less important half of it. A transducer can be examined and a frequency can be listened to, but nobody outside reads the attack tables, and the tables are where the years went. Set, depth-charge battery and school were drawn as one article, on the principle that the ship is the sonar's delivery system and not the other way about. The first generation is a conventional active and passive fit with modest ranges and nothing startling in it. The second is a scanning set that finds depth as well as bearing and produces an output good enough to aim a pattern thrown ahead of the ship. The equipment is level with foreign work, and the difference is the sea time these operators have had under a fisheries-protection heading nobody abroad questioned.

| Sonar | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp31snr] | Active 17 kHz: 2,000 m reliable / 3,500 m good conditions · passive hydrophone array |
| [jp43snr] | Scanning set: 4,000 m · depth-finding · ahead-throwing fire-control output |

### 4.10 Aircraft catapult and crane — Type 29 (1929)

The rating was fixed in advance of the aircraft that would use it, which is either good judgment or good luck. Catapult and crane share that rating and come off one drawing, so any cruiser or tender in the fleet can launch and recover anything the fleet flies from water. The launch is compressed air along a track and the recovery is an electric crane, and there is nothing exotic in either. Cruisers have carried both from new, and the carriers were given catapults only years afterward. The crane is quietly the more useful half, because float squadrons are hoisted in and out by it and a strike aircraft on floats has no other way home. A rating chosen that early, for an aircraft nobody had yet drawn, should not have held, and it did.

| Fitting | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Compressed-air track catapult and electric recovery crane, both rated 7.7 t · one drawing, all hulls |
| [jp29cat] | **Type 29** — 7.7 t to 105 km/h in 22 m · 1 launch every 45 s · crane 7.7 t at 12 m outreach · catapult 42 t, crane 26 t |

### 4.11 Fleet homing beacon — Type 31 (1931)

Overwater navigation is a fleet property in this navy and not a pilot's private problem. A ship transmitter works one fleet wavelength, every aircraft carries a receiver and a transponder, and the identification is keyed. A crew that can hear the net can find its deck, so a search runs to the limit of fuel rather than of a navigator's nerve. The transponder answers interrogation as well, which lets a ship's plot tell its own returns from somebody else's. The obvious weakness is that a beacon shines in both directions, and how hard that has been thought about is not something an outsider can establish. It is the least impressive article this navy fields and close to the most valuable.

| Beacon | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | One fleet wavelength · ship transmitter, aircraft receiver and transponder · keyed identification |
| [jp31bcn] | **Type 31** — ship beacon 300 km · aircraft receiver and transponder, interrogated to 130 km · ship set 340 kg, aircraft set 26 kg |

## 5. Conversion kits

### 5.1 Escort-carrier conversion kit — Type 31 (1931)

Any yard that can weld a block can do this conversion, and not one naval slipway is touched by it. The kit is less a design than the last stage of one, because the standard freighter was drawn with flight-deck foundations already in her. Those foundations were priced into the hull like a corrosion allowance, years before the kit was drawn and before the aircraft meant to fly off it existed. The conversion is quick, wants no naval yard, and therefore competes with nothing that is already building. The hulls sit in the merchant registry in plain sight and no attaché counts them, because until somebody decides otherwise they are honestly freighters. It is the largest single piece of naval mobilization anywhere in the world, and it is filed under shipping.

| Kit | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp31kit] | Escort-carrier conversion in ~90 days at any block-capable yard |

### 5.2 Hibari float kit — Type 33 (1933)

This kit taught the fleet that an aircraft's configuration is a stores question and not a design one. Floats, a crate, a few riggers and the ship's crane are the whole of it, and the exchange is done at sea in calm water. The wheels stow in the crate the floats came out of, which is the trick the kit turns on: nothing is left behind ashore. The penalties afloat are real and nobody pretends otherwise: the aircraft is slower, climbs worse and does not reach as far. It is also the configuration the heaviest sortie flies in, because water gives a takeoff run that a deck cannot. For an article so plain it changed where this navy believes its aircraft live.

| Kit | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp33flt] | Attach/detach at sea: calm water, ship's crane, 4 riggers, ~3 h · wheels stow in the crate · penalty afloat +280 kg · −50 km/h · −2.5 m/s climb · −15% radius |

### 5.3 Tenzan float kit — Type 39 (1939)

A tender, a swept anchorage and a stack of crates are a strike force without a flight deck. The kit puts the strike twin on floats in sheltered water, using any aviation crane in the fleet, and the wheels stow in the crate. The recess still carries the full ordnance set from water, torpedo included, which is the whole reason the kit was drawn. What it cannot do is come home to a deck: takeoff is from water or a catapult, alighting is on water, and recovery is by crane. The penalties are the heaviest any Japanese type accepts, and a squadron flying this way surrenders speed, climb and reach in quantities no pilot will enjoy. Float squadrons answer to the fleet train and forward anchorages, not to the carriers, which is a different way to run an air arm and not a worse one.

| Kit | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp39flt] | Attach/detach in sheltered water: any 7.7 t aviation crane, 8 riggers, ~5 h · wheels stow in the crate · water or 7.7 t catapult takeoff · alighting on water; crane recovery — no deck recovery · penalty afloat +720 kg · −90 km/h · −4 m/s climb · −20% radius (line ended 1944) |

## 6. Ordnance & drop tanks

### 6.1 Torpedo — Type 24 (1924)

Everything else this navy builds exists to deliver this. Its weight was fixed at what a single-engine aircraft lifts off a deck run, and half the navy was sized around that decision: tubes, recesses and hoists alike. The fish is stowed inert and charged from the ship's cascade at loading, and its warhead is built as modular charges that turn up elsewhere in the stores. The early generations had to be released slow and low, which is where an aircrew least wants to be over a defended target. The last one fixes that with a reinforced nose cap and a jettisoning tail shroud, and adds pattern running and an optional passive acoustic head. One torpedo serves every tube and every aircraft in this navy, and it is a fine weapon that is not the finest of its kind afloat.

| [jp24tor] | Enriched air: fast 40 kt / 6,500 m · slow 28 kt / 14,000 m (line ended 1933) |
| [jp33tor] | Oxygen: fast 49 kt / 14,000 m · slow 32 kt / 32,500 m |
| [jp45tor] | Second-generation air vessel and drive (+7% energy, settings held synced): fast 49 kt / 15,000 m · slow 32 kt / 35,000 m · improved depth-keeping · pattern-running gyro · passive acoustic homing head option, 28 kt terminal seek · drop-hardened release (reinforced nose cap, jettisoning tail shroud): ≤650 km/h / ≤250 m — homing head ≤550 km/h |

### 6.2 14 cm shell — Type 29 (1929)

A cavity for a fuze that existed nowhere on earth was cut into the first drawing of this shell. The fuze itself came more than a decade later, went into that cavity, and changed nothing about how the shell flies. One projectile serves surface action, anti-aircraft fire and shore bombardment across the whole fleet, so there is no wrong round to send up a hoist. The charge is semi-fixed and the fuze well takes impact or mechanical time, which is the entire choice a gunner has. The proximity generation is the same shell with the same ballistics and the same tables, so a ship re-ammunitions and her gunnery does not change a page. The filling is high explosive and nothing else, which is fine against aircraft and thin plate and useless against anything with a belt.

| Shell | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp29shl] | 40 kg high-explosive · semi-fixed charge · universal fuze well: impact / mechanical time · proximity-ready cavity |
| [jp42shl] | Proximity fuze in the same cavity, same ballistics |

### 6.3 650 kg bomb — Type 32 (1932)

There is no other bomb in this navy, so a loadout is settled on a flight deck rather than in a design office. It was drawn backward from a torpedo already frozen, because a tandem pair had to fill the space, the stations and the weight of one fish. It rides in pairs in the torpedo-form recess and singly on the bomb-form centerline station, and it needs no adapter to do either. The filling is semi-armor-piercing and the fuze well is the fleet's universal one, so it takes the same fuzes as everything else an aircraft drops. Having a single nature means a target that wanted blast gets a piercing bomb anyway, and that is a real limitation nobody here seems to mind. Nothing about the weapon is clever and everything about its shape is.

| Bomb | Specification |
|---|---|
| [jp32bmb] | 650 kg semi-armor-piercing · universal fuze well · pair-shaped in tandem to the torpedo-form recess · single carriage on the bomb-form centerline station |

### 6.4 Depth charge — Type 32 (1932)

Explosive production here is one stream feeding two shapes. The drum was sized around a charge that already existed, one of the modular sections of a torpedo warhead, so making depth charges fills nothing new. A nose fairing and a tail cone turn that same drum into an air store, carried several to a centerline station by anything with a bomb-form fitting. The first generation is a hydrostatic fuze across a shallow set of settings, which was adequate against the boats of its day and would not be now. The second reaches a good deal deeper and matches its thrower patterns to the scanning sonar, which makes it a setting and not a new store. The weapon is dull, and the way it is filled and stowed is the best single argument for the whole method.

| Depth charge | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | **One drum serves both generations** — 154 kg, 100 kg burster, that burster being one of the torpedo warhead's modular charges · air kit to the bomb form: nose fairing + tail cone, +8 kg per charge, **162 kg as dropped** · 4 per bomb-form centerline station |
| [jp32dpc] | Hydrostatic fuze 30–90 m |
| [jp42dpc] | 150 m setting · thrower patterns matched to [jp43snr] |

### 6.5 Mine — Type 32 (1932)

Held unassembled, the mine reserve and the torpedo reserve are the same inventory. A mine here is a warhead section married to a case kit, and until the two are put together neither object is a mine at all. That is why no registry inspection has counted them, and why the cases appear in no naval budget: they are sheet-metal work placed with civilian fabricators. The weapon itself is a moored contact mine laid over stern rails, and a drogue-retarded case in the bomb form lets aircraft lay it too. The later generation is an influence kit, magnetic or acoustic, fitted to the same case, so the stockpile need not be replaced to gain it. The mine is an ordinary weapon and its accounting is the remarkable part, because any foreign estimate of how many Japan holds is an estimate of the wrong thing.

| Mine | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Air kit: drogue-retarded cylinder case in the bomb form |
| [jp32min] | Moored contact · 660 kg laid (300 kg warhead section + case kit) |
| [jp44min] | Influence kit (magnetic/acoustic) on the same case |

### 6.6 Recess ferry tank — Type 39 (1939)

Fit is by shape here and not by aircraft type, so any airframe drawn later inherits these tanks without a new drawing. The tank is the standard bomb's outline in light alloy, which means it goes wherever that bomb goes and needs no station of its own. Because the recess drags the same whether the tank is full, empty or absent, the performance tables needed no new page when the tanks arrived. They are reusable and are brought home rather than dropped, and jettisoning a dry one is worth only a slight improvement in climb. A torpedo-form recess takes a tandem pair, or a bomb and a tank, which is how the strike twin flies the outer search with a weapon aboard. No item here is less glamorous, and few show more plainly what the whole method was for.

| Tank | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | One standard ordnance form — 650-form: 500 L in the 650 kg bomb form · 40 kg dry · fits any bomb-form centerline or recess station; a torpedo-form recess takes a tandem pair, or a bomb and a tank |
| [jp39tnk] | Reusable light-alloy · recess drags alike loaded or empty · retained for landing · dry jettison ~2% of climb |
