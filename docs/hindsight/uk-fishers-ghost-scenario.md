## 1. Thesis

**Speed is armor, and the Royal Navy has followed that gospel off a cliff and into legend.**

Fifteen years of compounding speed-worship have produced the fastest capital ships that will ever burn oil — hulls no enemy can force to fight and no Treasury can afford in numbers. The fleet is magnificent. **There is not enough of it. There was never going to be.**

The player inherits a navy that has been right about one thing for thirteen years and is about to discover which decade it was right about. The question on the desk is on a single budget line and the 1936 Estimates put it there: **the same money buys hulls five and six of the Incomparable line, or twelve cruisers and twenty-four destroyers.** The fleet's whole thesis says capital speed. The Empire's convoy maps say otherwise. Nothing in the file resolves it, and the file is honest that nothing can.

## 2. Strategy

### 2.1 Build sequencing — "the shed before the ship"

Every generation of British capital ship since 1927 was made possible by a **shore plant** that broke first. The Engineer-in-Chief's department runs full-scale boiler rooms at Haslar at pressures nobody has put to sea, fails them on land at the Admiralty's expense, and only then draws a ship around what survived. **The lead is in the failure rate and not in any invention**, which is why nobody has copied it and why nobody can quickly buy it.

The consequence for sequencing is that the machinery generation, not the hull, sets the cadence: the Insuperables could not be ordered until the 400 psi plant had two years ashore behind it, and the Board quadrupled the machinery vote in the same session that approved them **because the vote was the binding constraint and had been since 1922**. From 1930 the constraint moves to steel and stays there.

### 2.2 The Fisher school doctrine

**Engagement on the fleet's own terms, and the terms are a speed.** A ship that cannot be brought to action chooses the range, chooses the moment and chooses whether the action happens at all; a fleet of such ships is, in the school's phrase, *a decision the enemy is not permitted to make*. Everything else in this navy is downstream:

- **No torpedo on any capital ship since 1921.** A ship that will never be inside 10,000 m does not need one.
- **No provision for anything, anywhere, ever.** A British hull is worked for the equipment fitted on the day she commissions and nothing else, so every later fit is structural work at structural prices. ⚠ **This is the exact inverse of the Japanese divergence and it is not an accident of poverty — it is a position:** provisioning for a set you cannot describe is money the machinery vote could have had.
- **Armor is not traded, and the belt never moved.** The G3 scheme of 1922 is carried unaltered on every capital hull since: 356 mm, four generations, thirteen years. What each generation buys is speed, and it is bought in machinery and in length.
- **Trade defense has atrophied to lectures.** Hunting groups, division actions and breakoff drills are exercised to a standard nobody else approaches. Convoy is a syllabus item.

### 2.3 Personnel — 99 percent, and the wrong 1 percent

**Manning stands at 99 percent of establishment**, which is the best figure in the fleet's history and is quoted in every Estimates debate. It conceals a famine: the machinery generations that are the scenario's whole asset are maintained by **Engine Room Artificers and stoker-mechanics who take five years to train, and there are about 400 of them in the world qualified on the Mark IV plant.** Every high-pressure casualty in the fleet queues for the same few hundred men.

The fourteen capital hulls scrapped or sold between 1924 and 1931 released their crews into a fleet that grew by ten hulls, which is why the establishment fills. The Australian sale was the cleanest of the fourteen: ***Barham* and *Malaya*, RAN crews, and a Dominion battle squadron for the Far East on somebody else's payroll.**

### 2.4 Speed and range policy

**The fleet is banded and it is banded by design.** The fast divisions work at 36 knots; Hood, Renown and Repulse at 33 are *the slow ships* and are told so; the Counties at 32 are not in the fast divisions at all and work the distant stations alone. **A fleet organized by speed is a fleet that cannot concentrate**, and the Admiralty's own tactical publications have said so since 1931 in a footnote.

Range is the machinery vote's hidden bill. A 36-knot ship at anything above cruising speed empties like a bath, and the answer is not bunkerage — it is the **abeam refueling rig** [uk30kit], drawn in 1929, fitted from new in every capital ship laid down since 1927 and **fitted to nothing older, because that would be structural work.** It is the reason the fleet train doubled to 16 oilers and the reason the fleet's radius is an ocean rather than 900 kilometers. ⚠ **It is the single most consequential piece of equipment in the British catalog that nobody in the Admiralty regards as equipment.**

### 3.2 The build, 1922–1935

**The construction budget runs on two votes and only one of them is limited by anything.** `build_model_uk.py` audits the authored program against both; its rows are END OF YEAR and its output is `data/derived/uk_build_model.json`.

| | Vote | 1922 | 1930 onward | Limited by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hull** | tons of new construction | 54,000 t/yr, flat | 54,000 t/yr | Washington, First London, and the House of Commons every March |
| **Machinery** | shaft horsepower of new plant | 165,000 shp/yr | **680,000 shp/yr** | ⚠ **nothing** |

⚠ **The hull vote is about 1.6 times Britain's historical construction vote, and the four canon savings pay for nearly all of it** — fourteen capital hulls off the books between 1924 and 1931, twenty-three cruisers not built, about forty destroyers not built, and no modernization of anything, ever. **What remains is a few thousand tons a year, and §2.5 of the frame carries it without help:** Parliament is in open revolt over the estimates and the fleet consumed the 1920s. ⚠ **Three hulls were cut to bring the vote down to 54,000 t/yr — §5.3 names them, and §5.4 states the fleet that survives.**

**And the binding constraint moves, which is the model's finding.** Cumulative machinery demand runs at **128 percent of the vote in 1922** and peaks at **133 percent in 1923**, and the machinery vote binds every year from 1922 to 1926 — which is *why* it was quadrupled. From 1927 it is the **hull vote** that is over-subscribed, in **1927 through 1933 without a break**, ⚠ **and the machinery vote is never over-subscribed after 1929.** Cumulative hull load peaks at **105 percent in 1933** and ends the period at 94; the machinery vote ends at 80.

⚠ **That is why every generation grows and nothing ever comes off.** The G3's protective scheme of 1921 is carried unaltered by all four classes, so the four knots are paid for out of the machinery vote (160,000 shp to 244,000) and the 18-inch, the deeper torpedo system and Incorrigible's anti-aircraft battery are paid for out of the hull vote. **A navy that never deletes armor has to buy every improvement twice: once in steel and once in the plant to push the steel.**

## 4. National state

### 4.1 Personnel — the governor nobody sees

**About 400 artificers in the world are qualified on the Mark IV plant**, and the number is the fleet's real order of battle. Manning is at 99 percent, the establishment fills, and the training pipeline for a five-year trade cannot be sprinted. A war that puts four Insuperables, two Incomparables and an Incorrigible into sustained steaming at once puts every one of those men to sea simultaneously, and takes the training pipeline with them.

### 4.2 Inputs — the countdown

**Oil: a war reserve of 3.5 months at projected war consumption**, and the projection assumes the fleet steams as it exercises. It does not assume 36 knots. ⚠ **Every barrel is imported**, and the whole of the fleet's strategic mobility rests on 16 oilers and a rig.

**Armor plate:** capacity is modest and has been since 1922, because nobody ordered thick belts in fifteen years and three mills closed. A decision to armor anything would take four years to become steel.

**The 18-inch gun:** one works, one set of jigs, no second source, and a shell no other navy makes. It is the 1917 bore relined, which is why Britain could go up a caliber without a development vote — and why there is exactly one shop in the country that can cut it. **One factory fire disarms the flagship class**, and the Ordnance Board has written to the Board about it every year since 1932. Eighteen barrels depend on it now, and thirty-six will when the two Incorrigibles commission.

## 5. Rulings

⚠ **A ruling states what is true, and the file it lands in is the one that owns the fact.** The road file owns Britain's treaties, dates and the ledger; this file owns the fleet, the rulings and the opening decisions.

### 5.1 A British class heading year is the year she was ORDERED

⚠ **The as-launched sheet is the first ship to commission carrying the outfit set out on it**, and the two dates coincide only in Japan, where a class is *named* for its year of introduction. A British capital ship commissions three to five years after she is ordered: **the Incomparables are the 1931 program and their sheet is 1934; the Sabres are the 1922 program and their sheet is 1926.**

The data expression is §9.5 — `as_launched_year` and **no `type_year` at all**, because a Mark number is a serial and not a date — and `tools/validate.py` enforces the as-launched discipline against it. The platform catalog states the same rule in its header, where a reader meets the sheets.

### 5.2 The two Queen Elizabeths sold to the Royal Australian Navy are BARHAM and MALAYA

⚠ *Barham* becomes **HMAS Australia** and *Malaya* becomes **HMAS Anzac**, sold in 1926 with RAN crews. `data/ships/hindsight/uk_fishers_ghost_pack.json` retains `hms_barham` and `hms_malaya` from the base file; **Queen Elizabeth, Warspite and Valiant are the three scrapped by 1931.**

**Why those two, and it is a Board decision rather than a coin toss.** A Board purging everything slow sells the hulls it was never going to spend money on. In our history the Admiralty gave *Queen Elizabeth*, *Warspite* and *Valiant* full 1930s reconstructions and gave *Barham* and *Malaya* partial refits it never finished — **so the two sold here are the two that were, in fact, worth least to the people selling them.** And *Malaya* was the gift of the Federated Malay States and had never cost the Admiralty a penny: ⚠ **the Board parts with the one it never rebuilt and the one it never paid for**, which is exactly the transaction §2.5's Parliament would wave through.

**Two names were deliberately not used.** *Warspite* appears twice in `docs/sp-scenario-catalog.md` §3.3 as a 1940 set-piece combatant and cannot also be in Australian hands from 1926; and selling *Queen Elizabeth* would leave the Royal Navy's own class named for a ship in somebody else's navy.

### 5.3 The hull vote is 54,000 t/yr and the program was cut to fit it

⚠ **`HULL_VOTE` is a flat 54,000 t/yr, about 1.6 times Britain's real construction vote, and the program was cut to fit it rather than the vote raised to cover the program.** ⚠ **The peak load is set by the EARLY hulls, not the late ones**, which is why the cut fell where it did: *Unapproachable* and *Incorrigible* are laid down in 1935 and stand at 5 and 8 percent at the end of it, so canceling them saves a few thousand tons and does not move the vote by a single ton.

- **One Invincible** — *Inflexible*, the third G3, never ordered. **Class of two.**
- **One Insuperable** — the fifth of that program, never ordered. **Class of four.**
- ***Unapproachable* canceled** — the fourth Incomparable, laid down in 1935 and dropped at 5 percent, the marginal hull the Board parts with first.

⚠ **INCORRIGIBLE SURVIVES, AND DELIBERATELY.** She is 8 percent complete and nearly free, she is Britain's whole answer to naval aviation, and she is opening decision #6 — the scenario asking the player whether any of this was ever true. **Cutting the cheapest hull to save the best question is the wrong trade.**

⚠ **The model's finding, on the program as it now stands: the binding constraint MOVES.** Cumulative machinery demand runs at **128 percent of the vote in 1922 and peaks at 133 percent in 1923**, and the machinery vote binds every year from 1922 to 1926 — which is *why* it was quadrupled. From 1927 it is the **hull vote** that is over-subscribed, in **1927 through 1933 without a break**, and ⚠ **the machinery vote is never over-subscribed after 1929.** Cumulative hull load peaks at **105 percent in 1933** and ends the period at **94 percent**; the machinery vote ends at 80. **The program fits, barely, and only because nothing in it was ever refitted.**

### 5.4 The battle line is one ship, built four times

**The drawing office motto cut over the door at Bath in 1931 is the Controller's minute of 3 May 1922: *NOT FAST ENOUGH*.** It is a speed instruction and not an armor one, and the belt below is the reason it could be given at all.

**The Invincibles are the G3s as the Admiralty actually drew them in 1921, built without a line altered** — 48,400 t, 9 × 16 in in three triples, **2 forward and 1 amidships**, a 356 mm belt, 203 mm over the magazines, 32 knots. **Two hulls.** The Nelson class is never ordered, and ⚠ **these are the only British capital ships that carry a torpedo** — two submerged 24.5-inch tubes, drawn in 1921, struck from every design after.

⚠ **AND NOTHING EVER COMES OFF AGAIN.** Every class after her carries the G3's protective scheme unaltered in every dimension, so every generation's growth is paid out of the votes:

- **Insuperable (1927), four hulls** — the Invincible, four knots faster. 51,500 t, 244,000 shp, 36 kt, the same nine 16-inch on the same rings. ⚠ **ALL THREE GROUPS FORWARD — the Fisher arrangement adopted here and carried by every British capital ship afterwards**: a citadel 20 m shorter, which is most of what the four knots cost, and the astern arcs given up to buy it. **Declared at 35,000 tons and 29 knots**, which is where the speed lie starts and where 36 knots arrives to stay.
- **Incomparable (1931), two hulls** — the Insuperable with a bigger hole in the barrel. 54,500 t, 251,000 shp, 36 kt, **nine 18-inch Mark III**, the 1917 bore relined, same belt, same deck, same arrangement. Both in commission; *Indestructible* was never ordered and *Unapproachable* was canceled on the slip at 5 percent.
- **Incorrigible (1935), two hulls** — the fourth statement of the same ship and the first that answers a different question. 59,000 t, 265,000 shp, 36 kt, **the same nine 18-inch but all three groups forward**, and the twenty meters of citadel that saves, plus four and a half thousand tons the Board found in the 1935 estimates, goes into ⚠ **a 254 mm two-layer deck, a 9-meter liquid-loaded torpedo system of five bulkheads, and twenty-four dual-purpose 4.5-inch barrels, eighty pom-pom barrels and six high-angle directors — more anti-aircraft weight than the rest of the Royal Navy carries put together.** She was specified by two officers who spent 1933 and 1934 watching Fleet Air Arm exercises. **Britain's answer to naval aviation exists, it is correct, and it is 8 percent built.**

⚠ **The heavy-gun ladder is three Marks, all triples:** the 16-inch Mark I `[uk22gun]`, the 16-inch Mark II `[uk27gun]`, and the 18-inch Mark III `[uk31gun]`. **The line tops out at a caliber Britain historically owned**, so it claims nothing under conventions §12.

**Ark Royal receives a 64 mm armored flight deck over the hangar box**, paid for out of the lower hangar: 25,400 t, 169,000 shp, 3,300 m² of hangar and 51 aircraft embarked where the first sketch carried 66. **Three hulls** — Ark Royal, Pegasus, and *Perseus* 22 percent complete on the slip. **The Fleet Air Arm's three types enter service in 1936, 1937 and 1938**, so the carriers commission with nothing modern to fly. ⚠ **And Britain has no aero-engine SKU at all**, because the Admiralty buys airplanes and not engines; each aircraft's powerplant is a literal spec on its own sheet.

⚠ **The ledger is `uk-fishers-ghost-road.md` §4**, which owns the treaties it is drawn from. What belongs here is the shape it makes: Britain is at her capital and carrier ceilings and at barely half her cruiser allowance, with **28 hulls against a stated requirement of 70**. **The ship list runs capital ships first**, Ark Royal at §1.5.

## 6. Opening decisions

1. **Numbers, or the next knot.** The 1936 Estimates fund *either* hulls five and six of the Incomparable line *or* twelve cruisers and twenty-four destroyers. **This is the scenario in one budget line**, and the fleet's whole thesis and the Empire's convoy maps give opposite answers.
2. **The 18-inch problem.** Second-source it — expensive, slow, and an admission — stockpile deep, or accept that a single works disarms two ships and soon four, since the Incorrigibles take the same bore.
3. **Air-defense debt, and it is the whole of the armor argument now.** Nine capital ships carry the anti-aircraft outfit of 1927 — a pom-pom or two, no high-angle director until 1938, no second weapon in any year. **The correct answer is drawn and it is 8 percent built.** Refitting one Incomparable to Incorrigible's battery is 14 months in dock, about 900 tons high up and roughly half a knot, and there are two of her in commission with a third on the slips. **Refit the line, or finish the one ship that was designed right and accept that the other nine cross the water as they are?**
4. **Sell the slow.** Hood, Renown and Repulse would fetch enough from Dominion or allied treasuries to fund Incomparable hull seven. Hulls-in-being against purity of doctrine, and the Australian precedent is eleven years old and worked.
5. **Three oceans, eleven ships.** Singapore wants three capitals, the Mediterranean four, home waters the rest, and the arithmetic does not close. **Which sea gets told the truth?**
6. **Incorrigible.** Eight percent built. Finish her as designed and own the fastest capital ship that will ever exist and the least survivable; re-order her with a real belt and lose four knots, two years and the doctrine's last argument; or scrap the frames and buy thirty escorts with the plate. ⚠ **She is the scenario asking the player whether it was ever true.**
7. **The escort question, which is not on any agenda.** There is no escort program, no escort design, no escort yard allocation and no escort in the 1936 Estimates. Starting one costs a capital hull and produces nothing anybody can photograph. **The Anti-Submarine Division has asked in the same three paragraphs every year since 1929.**

## 7. Standing pressures (emergent, always on)

- **Every dock period removes about 9 percent of the battle line, and so does a single torpedo hit.** ⚠ **Eleven capital ships fly the White Ensign** — two Incomparables, four Insuperables, two Invincibles and the three slow ships — **and eleven is not a number that survives arithmetic.**
- **The fuel ledger is a countdown**, and it runs faster the faster the fleet goes.
- **Cruiser scarcity means raiders feast** until a 36-knot answer arrives, and **a 54,500-ton answer to a 12,000-ton raider is the most expensive pest control in history.**
- **The propulsion lead decays.** It is the one thing every rival is copying at once, and the shore-plant method is public the moment anybody thinks to look at where the money went.
- **The artificer famine tightens** with every generation, because each plant is harder than the last and the trade takes five years.
- **Parliament.** The estimates are contested every March by members who can read a fleet list and count cruisers.

## 8. What the rivals will plan from 1 January 1936

⚠ **Nothing below has happened when play begins** (frame §1.3). There are no baked reactive programs in this scenario, and every rival navy is its plain historical self on 1 January 1936. **What follows is what the Admiralty's own staff expect the answer to be once the money exists — a pressure to play against, not a fleet already building.**

⚠ **THE TOGGLE GATE.** Where a rival is itself toggled on in the configuration being played, **none of this describes that rival**, and the assessment must be re-derived live: fleets compose, assessments do not (SIM-RULE 4). This section is therefore short by design, and its shortness is the ruling.

**The general answer, and the Admiralty's air staff have been putting it in memoranda the Sea Lords decline to circulate since 1932: the answer to a ship that cannot be caught is a weapon that does not have to catch it.** Aircraft and numbers. A 40-knot ship is immune to the stern-aspect shot and to a submerged boat, and immune to neither of those things from above.

**France** is the one rival whose answer is already visible and already funded: a fast capital ship of her own, in numbers Britain cannot match ship for ship in the Mediterranean, and a cruiser force built for the trade routes Britain has stopped defending. **Italy** answers differently and more cheaply — light forces, torpedo craft and aircraft in the narrow sea, where a 36-knot ship has nowhere to use 36 knots. Neither of them has to beat this fleet. **Both of them only have to make it come to a place where speed is not a currency.**

**The Soviet Union** is not a naval problem in 1936 and the Admiralty's assessment says so in one paragraph.

## 9. Data & schema implications

1. **Equipment, designations, provisions:** per `docs/equipment-conventions.md`; British SKUs in `data/equipment/uk_fishers_ghost.json`. ⚠ **Britain has NO engine-cartridge SKUs.** Her machinery rows take the non-modular form — plant · shp · speed · range @ cruise — and the cruising speed is stated directly in the range clause. **The machinery generation is a design standard, not a swappable article**, and a hull carries the one she was laid down with.
2. **Provisions are the interesting case and they are mostly ABSENT.** Every British class declares `provisions` with `count: 0` and `state: "reserved"` for the interfaces it does not have, which is how the schema records **a navy that provisions nothing and pays structural prices forever.** The one exception is the abeam refueling trunk, fitted from new in capital ships laid down from 1927.
3. **National pack: `data/ships/hindsight/uk_fishers_ghost_pack.json`**, written in the component style. Base 1922 data (`data/ships/uk.json`) untouched, referenced and never modified.
4. **Build model: `build_model_uk.py`**, emitting `data/derived/uk_build_model.json`. ⚠ **It is an AUDIT, not a purse** — the program is authored and the model prices it against two votes. **Two votes is the schema implication**: `national_state.construction` needs a second envelope that is not tonnage. The model's class ids are the pack's ids exactly, and `tools/validate.py` holds the two against each other at END 1935: **BC 8 · CA 4 · CL 10 · CV 2 · DD 60 · SS 30.** The three converted large light cruisers and the three re-engined battlecruisers consume a vote and are **not** in that count — they are base-file hulls, and counting them here would count them twice.
5. **British sheets carry `as_launched_year` and no `type_year`.** A Mark number is a serial and not a date, so Britain has no type year in the Japanese sense; a class is named for the year she was **ordered** and commissions three to five years later. The as-launched discipline is enforced against `as_launched_year` — the year the first ship to commission with the outfit on the sheet did so — which is 1934 for the Incomparables, 1926 for the Sabres, and the design year for a ship that never commissions.
6. **The 18-inch single-source problem wants a data expression** — a `sole_source` flag on a component line, and a disruption event. Neither exists.
7. **The artificer famine is a `manpower.pools` entry with a five-year `training_months` and a count of about 400**, and it is the clearest case in the project for why that block exists.
8. **No `victory` block** for this scenario: readouts come from the standard indices.
9. **Fuel is the constraint this file leans on hardest and the one the schema does not yet consume.** 3.5 months, wholly imported, against a fleet whose consumption is a cube of its own doctrine.
