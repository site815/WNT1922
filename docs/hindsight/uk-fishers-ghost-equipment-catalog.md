# The Measured Mile — Equipment Catalog (Fisher's Ghost)

**Project:** WNT1922
**Version:** v20
**Companion to:** `docs/hindsight/uk-fishers-ghost-platform-catalog.md` (ships and aircraft — §1–§2, and the calibration position governing both halves) · `docs/hindsight/uk-fishers-ghost-scenario.md` (scenario — owns the order of battle and opening state) · `docs/hindsight/uk-fishers-ghost-road.md` (the 1919–35 timeline and the treaty ledger)
**Conventions:** `docs/equipment-conventions.md` — display law and band definitions.
**Designations:** British equipment is **Mark I–VI**, Roman, **one sequence per family in order of introduction** — a Mark number is a serial and not a date, and it does not tell you the caliber. Codes carry the `uk` prefix (`docs/equipment-conventions.md` §1).
**Scope:** §3 Weapons · §4 Sub-equipment · §5 Conversion kits · §6 Ordnance & drop tanks. ⚠ **There is no aero-engine line and there never was one: the Fleet Air Arm buys airplanes, not engines.** A British aircraft's powerplant is a property of the aircraft and is stated on its own sheet in the platform catalog. Section numbers run continuously with the platform catalog.
**Status:** Canonical design reference.

---

⚠ **THE THREE CONSTRAINTS EVERY ENTRY BELOW IS WRITTEN INSIDE.** This is a navy that spent more on marine machinery research than the rest of the world combined, bought nothing whatever to escort a convoy with, and stopped developing armor in 1922. An entry that does not answer to one of those three is answering to the machinery vote, which took what the others did not.

⚠ **One conventions note, and it matters more here than anywhere else.** Britain has **no engine-cartridge SKUs**. Her machinery is a **design standard, not a swappable article**: a hull receives the generation current on the day her keel is laid and keeps it for life, and the only way to change it is to cut the ship open. Britain has done that **three times in fourteen years** and it is §5.1. Every other navy's Machinery row is a menu; Britain's is a birth certificate.

## 3. Weapons

### 3.1 Heavy gun mounting — Mark I (1922)

The heavy gun is not where this navy put its money, and the ladder reads that way from end to end. Every mounting is hydraulic, bag-charged and conservative, and the newest of them is a wartime bore relined and put in a power mounting for the first time. Britain already owned the jigs for that caliber, which is the only reason a step up in bore needed no development program behind it. The bore is cut where those jigs are and nowhere else, and there is no second line to fall back on. Rate falls and mounting weight climbs as the caliber grows, and that surprises nobody who has handled a gun. Nothing in the line is bad gunnery and none of it leads anybody, and a fleet that spent fifteen years on turbines was never going to ask for more.

### 3.2 Secondary and cruiser mounting — Mark I (1924)

One heading covers weapons with almost nothing in common beyond a shellroom convention. The earliest is an open, hand-worked single that would not have surprised anybody in the last war, and it stayed the fleet's answer for years. The cruiser mounting after it is the only gun in the fleet drawn around a hull rather than the hull around the gun. Last comes an enclosed dual-purpose twin on a base ring, and it is the most advanced piece of surface ordnance Britain has. It weighs more than the job strictly demands, because somebody designed it properly instead of cheaply. The catch is where it went: into new construction only, because putting it in an older hull means cutting the ship open.

### 3.3 Multiple pom-pom — Mark I (1927)

A pom-pom throws a curtain and does not try to hit anything, and this one throws a respectable curtain. The gun is good — water-jacketed barrels on a common cradle, belt feed, and a rate that has nothing to apologize for. The director to work it took more than a decade to arrive, and until then the mounting was aimed by eye and hope. The later Mark changes nothing about the weapon itself and everything about whether it keeps firing, because the new feed jams a fraction as often. Against the airplanes in the air now the outfit is adequate. Nobody appears to have asked what it will be against the ones already on drawing boards, and the silence is worse than anything wrong with the gun.

|---|---|
| Common | Water-jacketed barrels on a common cradle · belt-fed · hydraulic power train · 80° elevation |
| [uk27can] | **Mark I** — 40 mm (2 pdr) · 8 barrels · 0.82 kg shell · 732 m/s · 3,500 m effective · 96 rpm/barrel · 140-round belts · 16 t |
| [uk39can] | **Mark II** — director-controlled from [uk32dir] · non-disintegrating belt, 1 stoppage in 4,000 rounds against 1 in 900 · no change to barrel, round, rate or mounting |

### 3.4 Torpedo mounting — Mark I (1925)

Everything a British flotilla can do to a battle line goes out in a single throw. There is nothing to put back in the tubes afterwards, in any destroyer the Royal Navy has built. The mounting itself is sound: centerline, powered right round, fired on a gyro angle set by the ship's own torpedo director. The later Mark adds a tube and changes nothing else, so calling it a new generation flatters it. Capital ships stopped carrying torpedoes long ago, on the reasoning that a ship which never closes to torpedo range has no use for them. The reasoning holds for the ships and leaves the destroyers carrying the whole burden with one load each.

| Mounting | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Deck mounting, centerline · powered train, 360° · fired on gyro angle from the ship's torpedo director · **no reload stowage** |
| [uk25lau] | **Mark I** — quadruple · 21 in (533 mm) · 26 t |
| [uk33lau] | **Mark II** — quintuple · 32 t |

## 4. Sub-equipment

### 4.1 Boiler and geared turbine — Mark I (1922)

Marine machinery is the one line in British naval procurement that is genuinely ahead of the world, and it is ahead by a long way. The lead is not an invention but a method: full-scale boiler rooms built ashore and run until they failed, at the Admiralty's own expense. Failures on land are cheap and nobody outside Britain has shown much appetite for buying them, which is why the lead has held. Each generation raises pressure and superheat and gets lighter for the same power, and the newest of them is a different order of machine from the first. None of it is swappable, because a hull receives the generation current when her keel goes down and keeps it for life. A few hundred men in the world can nurse the latest plant, and every machinery casualty in the fleet queues for the same faces.

| Machinery generation | Specification |
|---|---|

| [uk22eng] | **Mark I** — 250 psi, saturated · single-reduction gearing · weight index **1.00** · 0.42 kg/shp·h at full power |
| [uk27eng] | **Mark II** — 400 psi, 370 °C superheat · weight index **0.80** · 0.36 kg/shp·h |

### 4.2 Director and rangekeeper — Mark I (1927)

Fighting at a range of the fleet's own choosing means shooting well beyond the horizon of anything shorter than a masthead. So the tower carries the biggest rangefinder that will fit, the table sits behind armor, and an observer in an airplane corrects the fall of shot into it. The second Mark adds a stable vertical element and solves through the ship's own maneuver, which matters to a fleet that turns hard and often. It is also the last generation any older hull received, since fitting a newer one means structural work nobody has authorized. The air-defense table arrives late and only in ships drawn after the argument was won, so the rest of the line fights aircraft with a gunnery system. Heavy gunnery control here is first-rate, and every fault in it is something nobody ordered.

| Director | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Director tower and Admiralty Fire Control Table in a protected space · aircraft spot correction into the table · one crew, one school |
| [uk27dir] | **Mark I** — 9.1 m coincidence rangefinder in the tower · 4.6 m in each turret · 11 t head |
| [uk32dir] | **Mark II** — stable vertical element · 12.5 m rangefinder · full solution through the ship's own maneuver · 14 t |
| [uk38dir] | **Mark III** — second table for the dual-purpose battery · tachymetric air solution · barrage control for [uk39can] |

### 4.3 Ranging set — Mark I (1938)

Ranging came first in the requirement and searching came second, which is backwards from how every other navy came to the same equipment. The reason is not mysterious, because the fleet's heaviest gun shoots much further than the tower can see. The first set works on a long wavelength, feeds the existing table and does the job asked of it. The later one is centimetric, ranges blind and puts a plan position display in front of an operator, which is a real step forward. Fitting either is structural work on any hull afloat, because no British ship carries a mast, a wiring run or a power margin for gear nobody had imagined. How many ships have actually been through that surgery is not something an outsider can count from a dockside.

| Ranging set | Specification |

| Common | Masthead aerials · Signal School manufacture · feeds the existing fire-control table |
| [uk38rad] | **Mark I** — 7 m band · air warning 95 km (formations) · surface 22 km · gunnery ranging ±110 m |
| [uk41rad] | **Mark II** — centimetric · surface 26 km · blind-fire ranging ±25 m · plan position indicator (PPI) display |

### 4.4 Asdic — Mark I (1926)

The best equipment of its kind in the world belongs to the navy with the least use for it. The first set has no recorder at all and leaves the operator holding a stopwatch, which was normal everywhere at the time. After that the bearing tightens, contact is held right in to the drop, and a chemical recorder does the plotting a man used to do in his head. The newest set determines depth and feeds an ahead-throwing weapon, and nobody anywhere has taken the art further. All of it goes into the destroyer flotillas and into nothing else, because there is nothing else in the fleet to put it in. A school that turns out the finest operators afloat and a fleet with nowhere to billet them is the trade-defense problem in miniature.

### 4.6 Aircraft catapult and crane — Mark I (1924)

Gunnery owns this fitting, and that single fact explains most of what it is. The airplane it throws is there to sit above a target and correct the fall of shot, so the gear counts as an extension of the director. The first version is a compressed-air track on a turntable, worked by boat-handling gear and a derrick, and it is unremarkable in every respect. The second is flush in a flight deck, throws a heavier airplane harder and cycles faster, and the fleet insists on calling it an accelerator. The crane is ship's boat gear doing a second job, thrifty enough until the sea gets up. It is competent equipment doing a job the Admiralty takes seriously for reasons that have nothing to do with flying.

| Fitting | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Compressed-air track catapult on a training turntable · derrick crane, ship's boat gear · gunnery department fitting |

## 5. Conversion kits

*§5.1 is not used. The re-engining of Hood, Renown and Repulse was one dockyard evolution on three inherited hulls, none of which is a class in this catalog, and it is stated where those ships are listed; section numbers are never reused, so §5.2 keeps its number.*

### 5.2 Abeam refueling rig — Mark I (1930)

Without this rig the entire machinery lead stops at the dockyard wall. A fleet that can run at high speed and cannot be fueled at sea has the radius of a coastal navy, whatever its turbines do. The gear is straightforward — a boom and saddle on the oiler, a receiving trunk on the warship, and steady steaming while the hoses are across. It goes in from new in every capital ship young enough to have been drawn with it, and into nothing older, because that would mean cutting steel. Not one older ship has had it done, which says more about this Admiralty than any gun in the Royal Navy does. Nobody here treats it as equipment at all, and it is the most consequential thing on the whole list.

|---|---|

## 6. Ordnance & drop tanks

### 6.1 Torpedo — Mark I (1925)

There has never been a British oxygen torpedo, and everything about this weapon follows from that. It is a wet-heater of the ordinary kind, with a contact pistol and no influence fuze in any generation. The middle Mark adds speed on the fast setting and the last one adds reach on both, and neither touches the warhead. That the newest of them advertises better depth-keeping tells anyone who cares to read it what the older ones were doing. Settings are made at the tube, which is fine at night against a battle line and a nuisance in anything more complicated. British research money went into boilers for fifteen years, and the fastest torpedoes in the world are consequently somebody else's.

### 6.2 Armor-piercing shell — Mark I (1922)

Development of the British heavy shell amounts to a reworked cap and a reworked filling. The body, the fuze pocket and the ballistics are untouched, so the newer round drops into the same hoists and needs no new tables. The rework gives the cap bite at much steeper angles of impact, which is where a shell fired from far off actually arrives. That is a real gain and it is the only one this family has ever recorded. There is no high-capacity heavy round and no bombardment nature anywhere in it, because the Royal Navy shoots at ships and has never considered anything else. The heaviest projectile comes out of a single works, and an outsider cannot tell how deep the stock behind it runs.

### 6.3 Depth charge — Mark I (1922)

Fifteen years pass between these two drums and almost nothing happens in them. The charge is the same charge to within a rounding error, and the case is the same case. What changed is the setting range, which now goes deep enough to reach a boat that has properly gone down. Patterns are matched to the newer asdic, so the drops land where the operator says the contact is rather than where a stopwatch guessed. Somebody must have asked for a heavier weapon, and whatever answer they got is not on paper anywhere an outsider can reach. This drum is the whole of British anti-submarine offense, and its stagnation is the clearest single measure of how little that side of the fleet mattered.

| Depth charge | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Drum · hydrostatic pistol · stern rails and two throwers |
| [uk22dpc] | **Mark I** — 190 kg · 130 kg charge · settings 15–75 m |
| [uk37dpc] | **Mark II** — 191 kg · 132 kg charge · settings to 150 m · pattern matched to [uk34snr] |

⚠ **FIFTEEN YEARS AND THE CHARGE DID NOT GROW, AND THAT IS THE COLD BAND WORKING RATHER THAN AN OVERSIGHT.** The Mark I of 1922 is the wartime Type D — 190 kg with 130 kg of TNT — and the Mark II of 1937 is 191 kg with 132 kg, which is the real Mark VII of **1939** to the kilogram. The Anti-Submarine Division asked for a heavier charge in 1928 and the money went into boilers. **A cold band that outbuilds history is a mis-filing, and these are the real figures.**

### 6.4 Aircraft torpedo — Mark I (1932)

The airframe came first here and the fish was worked backwards from it, which reverses how every other weapon in the fleet was specified. It shows: a slow torpedo with a small warhead and a short run, and a drop gate that asks the pilot to arrive low and unhurried. Against a merchant ship that is enough, and against a maneuvering warship it demands a great deal of the man flying. Only a contact pistol is fitted, so a near miss under a keel does nothing at all. The honest position is that this is not the Fleet Air Arm's best weapon and never was. That distinction belongs to the wireless set in an observer's cockpit and the corrections it passes back to the guns.

### 6.6 Mine — Mark I (1923)

British mining is defensive, declared and dull. The pattern is a moored contact drum, a published warning and an area somebody else's merchantmen have to steam round. The later Mark puts a magnetic unit inside the same case and lays it off the same rails, and the work is neat and unambitious. Nothing suggests that offensive mining in an enemy's own approaches was ever seriously wanted. No capital ship carries rails, and on a hull built to run at speed there is nowhere sensible to put them anyway. The mine is therefore a destroyer and submarine store in a navy that has better uses for both, and it shows every sign of having been left alone.

| Mine | Specification |
|---|---|
| [uk23min] | **Mark I** — 750 kg moored contact · 140 kg charge · 550 m mooring |
| [uk39min] | **Mark II** — magnetic influence unit in the same case, on the same rails |

### 6.7 Drop tank — Mark I (1934)

It exists for the observer and for nothing else. A gun that shoots beyond its own horizon is a hope without a man in the air above the target, and the tank is what keeps him there. The article is an Air Ministry pattern taken without alteration, in light alloy, thrown away dry and never brought back aboard. It hangs on the ordinary underwing rack and takes up stations the airplane would otherwise fill with bombs. The scout and the torpedo airplane can carry it and neither fighter can, because no fighter here has a rack, a crutch or the plumbing. That is Britain not provisioning again, and it means the best carrier fighter afloat cannot follow the airplanes it is meant to protect.
