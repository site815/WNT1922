## 1. The three treaties

The divergence does not begin with a refusal. It begins with a **signature**, and with a decision taken the same week about which half of the treaty to obey.

| | Signed | Binds | What it caps | America's answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Washington** | 6 Feb 1922 | to 31 Dec 1936 | capital ships (525,000 t, and 35,000 t / 16 in per hull) · carriers (135,000 t) · fortification status quo in the Pacific. Cruisers limited only *individually*; destroyers and submarines **not limited at all** | Signs. Scraps the battlecruisers on the slips, in front of the newsreels — and completes the six South Dakotas the same treaty required scrapped, declaring them at the ceiling. The count is honest from the first day and the displacements are halved |
| **First London** | 1929 | 1 Jan 1930 to 31 Dec 1936 | the categories Washington left open — cruisers, destroyers, submarines, by total tonnage; a hard qualitative ceiling aimed at one delegation; annual exchange of building programs | Signs. Then runs the ladder at full rate — and, the size lie having died with the locks, stops denying the ships and starts disputing the law |
| **Second London** | — | — | verified limits (§3) | Attends with an instruction written to be refused. Uses the fortnight |

Three facts follow from the table, and the American position rests on all three. *This section states them for itself, from the table above and from §2.*

**One — the crime is capital ships, and the crime is public by 1929.** Every other category America touches is either inside its cap or over it by neglect. The ladder is the only deliberate breach, and it is the largest single treaty violation of the interwar period by tonnage.

**Two — the tell was poured in concrete in 1922 and read seven years late.** The third locks bill passed in the same session as the signature, and nothing in any treaty limits a lock, a dock, a gun plant or an armor mill. A **44.0 m usable beam** is a public specification for a public works, and no ship any treaty permits is wider than 33 m. The confession sat in the appropriation for seven years before a British constructor did the arithmetic in print.

**Three — everything expires on 31 December 1936.** America gives notice in December 1934. Both treaties therefore die on the last day of the scenario's first year, and Article XIX's fortification freeze on Guam, the Philippines, Hong Kong and the covered Pacific islands goes with them (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.4). The player starts on a clock every rival staff can read — and, unusually, on a domestic clock running in parallel: the presidential election of **3 November 1936**, eight weeks before the treaties die, and an inauguration three weeks after they do (`us-five-term-tillman-scenario.md` §5.4).

## 2. Road to 1935 — timeline

### 2.1 1916–1922 — the accession, the program, and the signature

**1916.** Tillman elected on the 1916 naval program — *a navy second to none*, and the largest capital-ship authorization in American history. He is inaugurated on **4 March 1917**.

**December 1918.** The General Board's escalation memorandum circulates: **the plant sets the tempo, and the plant is the strategy.** Ships wear out; chambers, ways and mills do not. The memorandum is never made policy and has never needed to be.

**1919–21.** The 1916 program builds on. **South Dakota keels, 1920.** The Bureau's 18-inch study opens; Washington Navy Yard begins the first plant expansion. Tillman is re-elected in **1920**.

**1922. 6 February: the United States signs the Washington Treaty**, and the divergence begins the same afternoon. Refusing in 1922 would have meant an open race against Britain and Japan before a single 18-inch chamber existed; signature buys the one commodity the ladder needs, which is **years in which nobody else can afford to answer.** What the President signs and what the President does are separated by a single decision taken that week: the treaty requires the United States to scrap both the Lexington battlecruisers *and* the six South Dakotas. **The battlecruisers are scrapped on the slips** — filmed, newsreeled and genuinely destroyed (*"hangars for admirals,"* the President says, and the phrase kills the conversion study before it is written). **The South Dakotas are completed** and declared at the ceiling. The first act of the divergence is choosing which half of a treaty to obey, and the half chosen is the half that would have produced carriers. Langley commissions as the tolerated experiment. The **third locks bill** passes: the crusade begins.

### 2.2 1922–1929 — the crusade, and the lie that could be poured in concrete

**1922–1935.** The infrastructure crusade runs continuously underneath everything below — third locks, monster graving docks, gun plants tooled upward bore by bore, armor capacity multiplied fourfold. None of it is treaty-limited and none of it is a warship. Each dock and lock is sized for the generation after the one now building, and by 1929 that is no longer true of the locks.

**1923.** **Missouri, Ohio and Maine laid down** — the ladder's founding generation, eighteen months after the bombing trials and on the battery already in service, because the eighteen-inch is a drawing and a single forging and the doctrine forbids waiting. ⚠ **They are declared to the treaty registry as 35,000-ton, 16-inch replacement hulls, and the caliber is true.** Only the displacement is a lie, and it is roughly halved. The declaration is accepted on paper because there is nothing but paper to check it against, and because the one figure anybody could check is the one figure that is honest.

**1924.** The 18 in/48 proofs at Dahlgren. The South Dakotas complete into a fleet that suddenly looks small around them. Tillman is re-elected in **1924**.

**1925–26.** **Constitution, Constellation and Congress laid down** on the newly proved eighteen-inch — grown entirely in length, because the beam is the canal's and the canal will not move. The 5 in/25 high-angle standard; Astoria-type cruiser keels — the one line Congress funds without argument. Graving docks begin at Norfolk, Newport News and Bremerton. The press tour of the turret shop runs a full newsreel reel; the ladder is public theology at home and a line of numbers abroad, and for five years those are two different countries.

**1927–29.** **United States, President and Chesapeake laid down**, one a year — the third generation, on the founding bore because the twenty-one-and-a-half is not ready, and grown in every other dimension instead. **The design is the 1917 maximum-battleship study taken out of its cabinet and drawn to lock chambers that are still being poured.** They are declared as replacement hulls on the same arithmetic as the six before them, and by now nobody outside the Navy Department writes the declaration down without a remark.

**1928.** Tillman is re-elected. **Missouri commissions and her trials are clean**; the Bureau's craftsmen have made the extraordinary look routine, which is exactly what the next appropriation needs. The 21.5 in gun is a year from proof and the next generation is a year from its keels, which the escalation doctrine treats as an ordinary scheduling fact rather than as a problem.

**1929. The busiest year of the divergence, and the year it stops working.**

- **The 21.5 in/45 proofs at Dahlgren** — photographed from a hillside by a man with a Zeiss lens and a Baltimore address. **Tillman is laid down the same year**, the first hull in the world whose requirement names a mission instead of a weapon.
- **October: the crash.** The ladder re-justifies itself overnight as relief — *steel pyramids*, the papers say, approvingly and otherwise.
- **October: the leak.** A British constructor publishes the arithmetic nobody in Washington thought anyone would do: **the third locks are specified at 44.0 meters of usable beam**, the new graving docks are cut to match, and no declared American ship — nor any ship any treaty permits — is wider than 33.
- **December: Ranger.** Congress's aviation caucus extracts one carrier over a veto threat, and lays her down in December. No second unit follows.
- **The First London Naval Treaty is signed**, and it is aimed at one delegation: a hard 35,000-ton, 16-inch ceiling, caps on the categories Washington left open, and **an annual exchange of building programs**, because the 1922 numbers rest on each party's own word and the word has stopped being worth anything.

### 2.3 1930–1934 — the lie changes shape

**1930.** **Constitution commissions**, and the eighteen-inch goes to sea for the first time in any navy.

**And the size lie dies here, because it has to.** No declaration survives a 21.5-inch barbette, and one is already on a slipway. So the lie changes shape: **America concedes the hulls in public and disputes the law instead** — inside its aggregate on a replacement schedule, the per-hull qualitative limits treated as a separate and lapsing article, and from 1930 the new treaty's own **escalator** invoked as a matter of routine. It is not a lie, which is why it cannot be disproved, only disputed. The concealment moves to the **rate**: declared completion dates run years long, hulls are publicly suspended and quietly worked, and the ladder's true cadence is never stated anywhere. The new tell is the **armor mills**, and a mill has to be worked out from ore contracts and furnace capacity rather than counted from a hillside (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §3.3).

**1931.** **America laid down** — and the first hull whose declared displacement the Secretary's own draft could not make plausible; the President wrote the correction in pencil in the margin. The first annual program exchange is filed on time and believed by nobody; by 1933 the exchange is an annual insult conducted in good grammar. The Farragut destroyer program is authorized — and raided the next spring for monster overruns.

**1932.** Tillman is re-elected to a fifth term, which begins on 4 March 1933 and ends on 20 January 1937. Depression bottom; the monster-yard districts are the only ones hiring, and they vote like it. **The fifth-generation design opens against a graving dock that has already been specified at three hundred and fifty meters.**

**1933.** **Republic laid down**, the third and last of her generation. **United States commissions.** Farragut is raided a second time; eight boats survive of thirty-two planned.

**1934.** **The third locks open — usable beam 44.0 meters**, the largest concrete works on Earth, inspected by the world's press at a ribbon-cutting; the leak of 1929 is now a public monument. **President and Chesapeake commission through 1934 and into 1935** — the fleet finding their crews the only way it has ever found crews, out of the hulls they replace (`us-five-term-tillman-scenario.md` §4.1). It is the cascade's first and largest bite, and it takes the whole of the reserve. **In December the United States gives notice of withdrawal from both treaties, effective 31 December 1936.** Missouri's division shoots Fleet Problem XV's towed targets to splinters at ranges the umpires must certify by aircraft. Ranger commissions in June; her one air group's practice-bomb straddle of a towed hulk runs in four thousand theaters, and the Air Lobby is born as a newsreel. Farragut delivers with the new 5 in/38.

### 2.4 1935 — the fortnight

**January.** A Canal Zone engineer's memo lays Tillman's molded beam against the chamber drawings — **44.5 against 44.0** — and is filed without minute. **Tillman commissions in the spring, and does not use the canal again.**

**August.** The first of the enlarged 21.5-inch liners for the fifth generation cracks in proof turning.

**October–November.** **Columbia, Liberty and Independence laid down** on the new fifth-generation slips — Columbia's keel plate going on at Newport News on the 24th. The forging cracks again in November.

**9 December: the Second London Conference convenes.** The American delegation sails with no authority to sign and one instruction that reads as a negotiating position and functions as a stopwatch: **no ceiling below 142,000 tons, and grandfather recognition of every hull building.** It is not an offer. It is a floor written to be refused, and its purpose is the fortnight — every week the room spends arguing about ceilings is a week of fourth-generation plate and another quarter of 21.5-inch forging money. The delegation is expansive, courteous and immovable. Columbia's frames were already rising when the delegates sailed, and Liberty's while they argued.

It works for a fortnight. On **20 December France tables verification as a precondition** of any signature, an inspectorate with access to registries and building slips; on **22 December the American delegation seconds it**, on the public ground that after Singapore no Japanese declaration can be believed — and discovers within 48 hours that it has seconded a motion that would put a Frenchman in the Newport News mold loft. On **23 December 1935** Washington enters a reservation excluding hulls already building, Japan refuses outright, and the conference breaks down.

**The breakdown is 23 December 1935; play begins 1 January 1936** (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.4).


### 2.5 The ladder, keel by keel

⚠ **THE INTERVAL IS TWO YEARS AND THE BUILD IS SIX, AND THAT IS THE WHOLE ARITHMETIC OF THE AMERICAN PROBLEM.** Every figure elsewhere in this file and in `us-five-term-tillman-scenario.md` §3.1 is checkable against this table.

| Generation | Design | Keels | Commissioned | Keel to sea | Ways held |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Missouri** — Missouri, Ohio, Maine | 1923 | all 3 in 1923 | 1928–29 | **5 years** | 5 |
| **Constitution** — Constitution, Constellation, Congress | 1925 | 1925–26 | 1930–31 | **5 years** | 6 |
| **United States** — United States, President, Chesapeake | 1927 | 1927, 1928, 1929 | 1933, 1934, 1935 | **6 years** | 8 |
| **Tillman** — Tillman, America, Republic | 1929 | 1929, 1931, 1933 | 1935, 1937, 1939 | **6 years** | 10 |
| **Columbia** — Columbia, Liberty, Independence | 1932 | all 3 Oct–Nov 1935 | 1941–42 at current rate | **6 years** | 6 |

**Read the last two columns.** The design cadence has never once slipped: five generations in nine years, each larger than the last. **The keels have slipped in every generation since the second** — the founding three went down together in one year, the fourth's three are spread across five, and the fifth waited three years for a slip to come free. ⚠ **The ways, not the vote and not the treaty, are what the ladder is now running against**, and the fifth generation is the first whose dock was specified before the ship and of which there are exactly two.

⚠ **AND THE COLLISION IS ALREADY BOOKED.** Three Columbias complete in 1941–42, and *America* and *Republic* complete in 1937 and 1939 on the same yards and out of the same berth pool. **Five capital hulls finish inside five years, and the berths the five need and the berths the old fleet holds are the same number** (`us-five-term-tillman-scenario.md` §4.1).

## 3. Why Second London fails

The conference's entire purpose is a displacement ceiling — any number at all would do, because any number ends the ladder. Two treaties have already set one and Washington has broken both, so what France tables on 20 December is not a number but **verification**: an inspector in the Newport News mold loft, because after the locks opened no American declaration will ever be believed again.

That is the demand the American instruction was written to make impossible. Accepting no ceiling below 142,000 tons — the President will not orphan the generation on the slips — and demanding grandfather recognition of both fourth-generation hulls and both fifth is a floor that negates every ceiling, and everyone in the room knows the President wrote it personally. Britain cannot bless a 142,000-ton world without building one; Japan left the conversation years ago and attends to watch; France and Italy will sign nothing the other two won't.

And there is a second thing verification would find, which is not the ships. **It is the rate.** The hulls have been public since 1930 and the legal argument has been public with them; what no inspector has ever been allowed near is the armor mills' order book, and that is the number from which a staff could work out when the ladder actually lands. America is not protecting a secret fleet. It is protecting a schedule.

Collapse, 23 December. The ladder's political lesson survives the wreck — nobody ever again proposes a number to Washington — and so does the thing the fortnight actually bought, which was fourteen days of construction inside a conference convened to prevent it.

## 4. The treaty ledger at 1 January 1936

What the United States is permitted, and what the United States has. Permitted tonnages from §1; hull counts from the scenario's order of battle (`us-five-term-tillman-scenario.md` §3.1); standard displacements from the platform catalog, except the legacy hulls — the modernized and reserve Standards, the older dreadnoughts, Langley, the Omahas, the flush-deckers and the S-boats — which are historical figures stated here for themselves.

| Category | Permitted | Actual | Ratio | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Capital ships** | 525,000 t (Washington) · 35,000 t and 16 in per hull | ~**2,221,000 t** including the five building — **1,571,000 t on the register** | **423%** | The crime, and it began on 6 February 1922 with six South Dakotas that should have been scrapped. ⚠ **Only the founding generation is inside the qualitative limit on caliber, and it is outside it on displacement by a factor of nearly two.** |
| **Aircraft carriers** | 135,000 t (Washington) | Ranger 14,500 + Langley 12,700 = **27,200 t** | **20%** | The price. The one allowance America was handed free and never came near spending. |
| **Cruisers** | 323,500 t (First London) | 16 heavy + 10 Omaha ≈ **222,500 t** | **69%** | The one line Congress funds without argument, and it is inside the cap because the ladder keeps eating its appropriation. |
| **Destroyers** | 150,000 t (First London) | 8 Farragut + ~170 flush-deckers ≈ **214,300 t** | **143%** | A 1917 surplus the 1929 treaty required scrapped. Congress never funded the scrapping. A lazy defection, not a designed one — and the reason the modern screen is eight boats. |
| **Submarines** | 52,700 t (First London) | 14 Dolphin + ~41 S-class ≈ **58,600 t** | **111%** | Same shape, smaller number, same cause. |

**The capital-ship column, decomposed**, since it is the only figure anyone will want to check: on the register, 3 × 58,000 + 3 × 72,000 + 3 × 92,000 + 1 × 112,000 + 6 × 43,200 + 533,800 t across the eight modernized Standards, six reserve Standards and four older dreadnoughts = **1,571,000 t**. Building: 2 × 112,000 + 3 × 142,000 = **650,000 t**. "On the register" is the treaty basis and counts reserve and mothballed hulls; it is not a statement about how many of them have crews.

**Read the column, not the rows.** America is well over four times over in the one category the ladder is made of, and at **one-fifth** of an allowance it was given for nothing in the one category that would answer a carrier navy. The destroyer and submarine overages are not policy; they are old hulls nobody paid to cut up. The ledger is not the record of a nation cheating everywhere — it is the record of a nation that spent every dollar of defection, and every dollar of everything else, on guns.

**And the clock.** Every figure in the *Permitted* column becomes zero on **31 December 1936**, along with Article XIX's fortification freeze (§1). What the two treaties bought the ladder was **fourteen years of low rival appropriations** — years in which nobody else could afford to answer, whether or not they knew. That purchase expires in twelve months to the day. **What those fourteen years were worth depends entirely on who else was in the world, and that is a setup-screen question, not a fact of this file** (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.3 — assessments are read live against the active configuration and are never inherited).
