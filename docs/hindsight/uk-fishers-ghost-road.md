## 2. Road to 1935 — timeline

### 2.1 1919–1926 — the appointments, the eleven weeks, and the machinery vote

**1919–1927. Fisher does not survive to 1935 and does not need to.** He survives to 1927 — seven years past his historical death, lucid enough to win the argument and long enough to staff it — and what he spends those years on is **not ships but appointments**. The Controller's department, the Engineer-in-Chief's, the Naval Construction directorate and two successive First Sea Lords come out of the same school. By the time he dies the proposition *speed is armor* has stopped being a man's obsession and become **a career path** — an idea that can be promoted into, and therefore cannot be argued out of.

**1922. The signature, the eleven weeks, and the first steel.** The program is suspended on the afternoon of 6 February as the treaty requires, and resumed on **24 April** as "reconstructions" of hulls surrendered for scrapping. ⚠ **Two Invincible keels go down — the G3s as drawn in 1921, built without a line altered** — with the first Courageous conversion and the first of sixty destroyers, five a year for twelve years. They are declared at 35,000 tons and 29 knots. The world gasps and files the declaration; the Admiralty's only complaint is the speed. **The machinery vote binds from this year and stays binding for five**, at 128 percent of what it can fund.

**1922–24. The Engineer-in-Chief's department receives what it asked for**, which was not a design brief but **a research vote**, renewed annually and never once debated in the House, because a turbine is not a ship. The first full-scale shore boiler plant at Haslar is running by 1924, and the machinery load peaks at **133 percent of the vote in 1923** — the highest either vote reaches in fourteen years, and the reason the vote is quadrupled rather than trimmed. The submarine line opens in 1923 at three boats a year, and the first five destroyers commission in 1924.

**1924–29. Everything slow is purged to pay for what comes next.** The R class and the Iron Dukes are scrapped. ***Barham* and *Malaya* are sold to the Royal Australian Navy** as HMAS *Australia* and *Anzac*, with RAN crews — a Dominion battle squadron for the Far East **on somebody else's payroll** — and *Queen Elizabeth*, *Warspite* and *Valiant* are scrapped by 1931. **Fourteen capital hulls leave the upkeep and manning books in seven years.** The County class is ordered in four annual installments from 1924; it is **the last cruiser program Britain runs to anybody else's requirement**, and no British heavy cruiser is laid down after 1927.

**1925. The two Invincibles commission**, three years from keel to sea — the G3s as the Admiralty actually drew them, and **the only British capital ships that carry a torpedo.** The first converted Courageous joins them. The machinery load falls below 110 percent for the first time since the treaty.

**1926. A quiet year, and the last of its kind.** Five destroyers and three boats commission; the third County keel goes down. ⚠ **It is the last year in which the machinery vote is the binding constraint on anything.**

### 2.2 1927–1932 — the constraint moves, and the second rung

**1927. The binding constraint moves, and it never moves back.** The quadrupled machinery vote has done its work; from this year forward it is **the hull vote that binds, in 1927 through 1933 without a break**, and the machinery vote is never over-subscribed after 1929. ⚠ **That single fact is why the fleet has the shape it has: knots are affordable and hulls are not.**

**1927–32. The four Insuperables build and commission**, two keels in 1927 and one each in 1928 and 1929: **51,500 t, 36 knots on 244,000 shp**, and ⚠ **not one plate of the G3's protective scheme altered.** The requirement was one line — *the Invincible, four knots faster* — and everything the four knots cost was paid in machinery and in hull: fourteen meters of length, three thousand tons, and eighty-four thousand more shaft horsepower. **This is where the fleet's 36 knots arrives, and it never leaves again.** They are declared at 35,000 tons and 29 knots. ⚠ **ALL THREE GROUPS FORWARD — the Fisher arrangement adopted here and carried by every British capital ship afterwards:** a citadel twenty meters shorter, which is most of what the four knots cost, and the astern arcs given up to buy it.

**1927–31. Hood, Renown and Repulse are re-engined to 33 knots**, one hull a year, fourteen months in dock each, a third of the structure disturbed, and ⚠ **nothing else changed: no armor, no fire control, no anti-aircraft, no provision for anything.** It is a pure machinery-vote item — no new steel at all — and it costs more shaft horsepower than the entire County program. They emerge faster than they were and slower than everything laid down since, and are thereby, in a fleet where 33 knots is slow, **the slow ships.**

**1928–30. The Counties commission**, one in 1928, two in 1929 and one in 1930, and the heavy-cruiser line closes behind them. The third Courageous conversion completes in 1930.

**1929. The First London Naval Treaty is signed, and it carries a cap on capital-ship speed and shaft horsepower.** Britain signs it. **The Swift class is ordered the same year: 7,200 tons, the fleet's 36 knots, and a quarter of standard displacement in machinery, which no cruiser in any navy has ever carried. Twelve hulls, in six annual pairs** — two keels a year from 1929 to 1935, two commissioning a year from 1931. It is also the year Britain comes in at **half her cruiser allowance** without being asked to.

**1930. The Incomparables' design is signed, and the deception dies as a secret.** From this year **a declared tonnage can be argued about for a decade; a speed is measured from a destroyer's bridge with a stopwatch**, and 36 knots on 35,000 declared tons is not a lie anyone can maintain. From 1930 the deception is a formality nobody troubles to believe.

**1931–32. The last Insuperable commissions, the first Swifts join, and three keels go down that decide the rest of the decade:** two Incomparables and the first Ark Royal. ⚠ **Cumulative hull load passes 100 percent in 1931 and stays above it for three years.**

### 2.3 1933–1935 — the dream, the ship after it, and the fortnight

**1933. The peak.** Two Swifts commission and the hull load reaches **106.7 percent of its vote — the highest cumulative figure of the whole program.** Nothing is canceled; the overrun is carried, which is what a fleet that consumed a decade looks like on a ledger. It is also the last year the destroyer and submarine lines lay a keel: **sixty destroyers and thirty boats in eleven years, and not one escort, in any year, ever.**

**1934–35. *Incomparable* and *Inimitable* commission:** 54,500 t, **nine 18-inch in three triples, two forward and one amidships**, the fleet's **36 knots on 251,000 shp**, and the G3's belt of 356 mm, unchanged again. The gun is the 1917 bore relined, from one works with one set of jigs. ⚠ **The drawing office motto is cut over the door at Bath in 1931 and it is the Controller's minute of 3 May 1922 — *not fast enough*.** A third and fourth hull do not survive the vote: *Indestructible* was never ordered and *Unapproachable* was canceled on the slip at 5 percent.

**1934–35. *Ark Royal* and *Pegasus* commission** — 25,400 t and 36 knots, because a deck that cannot keep up with the battle line is a deck this fleet has no use for, and with **64 mm of armor over the hangar box**, the only new armor any British department has bought since 1922. It was paid for in airplanes: the lower hangar lost a fifth of its length and the class carries 51 where the first sketch carried 66. ⚠ **A third, *Perseus*, is laid down in 1935 and is 22 percent complete when the delegation sails — the only carrier hull on any British slip.**

**1935. The Incorrigibles.** Ordered in March, laid down in September, **8 percent complete when the conference collapses.** 59,000 t, the same nine 18-inch but **all three groups forward**, and the twenty meters of citadel that saves — plus four and a half thousand tons the Board found in the 1935 estimates — goes into ⚠ **a 254 mm two-layer deck, a 9-meter liquid-loaded torpedo system of five bulkheads, and twenty-four dual-purpose 4.5-inch barrels, eighty pom-pom barrels and six high-angle directors: more anti-aircraft weight than the rest of the Royal Navy carries put together.** They were specified by two officers who spent 1933 and 1934 watching Fleet Air Arm exercises. **Britain's answer to naval aviation exists, it is correct, and it is 8 percent built.**

At the year's end the cumulative hull load has fallen back to **99.5 percent** and the machinery load to **89.4** — the program fits, barely, and only because nothing in it was ever refitted.

**9–23 December 1935. Second London.** §3. The British delegation arrives with a government that cannot politically abandon two building Incorrigibles, a carrier on the slip and a battle line it cannot explain at all, and therefore cannot accept an inspector; it spends a fortnight proposing qualitative limits *everyone in the room knows are written around British ships*. It is not an offer; it is fourteen days of *Unapproachable*'s frames.

**France tables verification as a precondition on 20 December** — the one delegation with nothing to lose by an inspector — and the United States seconds it on the 22nd. Japan refuses outright; Britain enters a reservation on access to building slips that everyone in the room reads correctly; **the conference adjourns *sine die* on 23 December.** ⚠ **Per frame §1.4, no delegation in the room wanted a different outcome.**

**The breakdown is 23 December 1935; play begins 1 January 1936** (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.4).

## 3. Why Second London fails

Displacement and speed were capped in 1922 and again in 1929 and Britain has broken both, so what the conference actually convenes on is **verification** — a constructor in the Clydebank mold loft, because no British declaration has survived a stopwatch since 1930.

Britain cannot accept one, and the reason is not the capital ships, whose speed everybody has known for five years. It is that an inspector in a British yard would be standing in the one place the whole divergence lives — **the machinery shops**, where a research vote nobody has ever debated has been buying shore-plant proof for thirteen years. A qualitative limit is negotiable. A man with a clipboard at Haslar is not.

⚠ **And the reason verification was on the agenda at all was a fourth navy that was never in the room** — the June announcement, and the fact that Britain had legalised it herself. A signatory that has stopped behaving as though the room still exists is the first visible crack in it, and Britain made that crack in June and then spent December objecting to the consequences.

## 4. The treaty ledger at 1 January 1936

Permitted tonnages from §1; hull counts from the scenario's order of battle (`uk-fishers-ghost-scenario.md` §3.1); standard displacements from the platform catalog.

| Category | Permitted | Declared | Actual | Ratio | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Capital ships** | 525,000 t (Washington) | 378,100 t | 2 × Invincible + 4 × Insuperable + 2 × Incomparable + Hood, Renown, Repulse = **509,900 t** | ⚠ **97%** | The eight new hulls were each declared at 35,000 t, so she lied on every form. ⚠ **AND THE AGGREGATE IS LEGAL: she sits inside what Washington allowed her by fifteen thousand tons, a third of one hull.** **Britain's lie was never about tonnage — it was about speed, and it died in 1930.** |
| **Aircraft carriers** | 135,000 t (Washington) | 118,300 t | Ark Royal, Pegasus + Courageous, Glorious, Furious = **118,300 t** | **88%** | ⚠ **Under her allowance and declared honestly, in the one category she had no reason to cheat in.** Nobody has ever remarked on it. |
| **Cruisers** | 339,000 t (First London) | 179,200 t | 4 × County + 10 × Swift + ~14 older = **179,200 t** | **53%** | ⚠ **THE STRONGEST LINE IN THE LEDGER: 28 hulls against a stated trade-defense requirement of 70.** Britain did not build what the treaty permitted her to build, and the money went into the machinery vote. |
| **Destroyers** | 150,000 t (First London) | 117,000 t | 60 × Sabre + ~30 war-built = **117,000 t** | **78%** | One design repeated for fourteen years, and no escort, sloop or corvette in any year of the program. |
| **Submarines** | 52,700 t (First London) | 52,500 t | 30 × Sturgeon + ~15 older = **52,500 t** | **100%** | At the cap by accident. The submarine committee has met twice since 1929. |
| **Installed shaft horsepower** | ⚠ **NO CATEGORY EXISTS. NO TREATY LIMITS A TURBINE** | — | approximately **7,300,000 shp** in commission | — | **The category the treaty forgot, and the only one Britain never lied about, because there was nothing to lie to.** |

**Read the table twice.** Britain is a hair inside her allowance in capital ships, *under* in carriers, and **at half her allowance in the category the Empire's sea lanes actually need.** She is the only defector in the scenario who broke the treaty in the direction of having *less* fleet, and she did it in the categories that would have mattered.

The four canon savings — fourteen capital hulls off the books between 1924 and 1931, twenty-three cruisers not built, about forty destroyers not built, and no modernization of anything, ever — pay for nearly all of a hull vote about 1.6 times Britain's historical construction vote. **What remains is a few thousand tons a year, and frame §2.5 carries it without help:** Parliament is in open revolt over the estimates and the fleet consumed the 1920s.
