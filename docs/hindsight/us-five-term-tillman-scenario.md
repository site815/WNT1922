## 1. Thesis

President Tillman — inaugurated 4 March 1917, fifth term ending 20 January 1937, and running again this November — never met a battleship big enough. **A new and larger generation has gone down the ways every two years since 1923**, each laid down before the last was evaluated: **fifty-eight, seventy-two and ninety-two thousand tons standard worked up and at sea, a hundred and twelve thousand commissioning and fitting out, a hundred and forty-two thousand on the slips.** Each class was, on completion, unarguably the most powerful gun platform on Earth, and the industrial base beneath them — locks, docks, gun plants, armor mills — is Tillman's true monument and the Depression's favorite jobs program. The price was paid in one currency: fourteen years of naval aviation famine. The question on the player's desk, asked in an unsigned Bureau memo, is the scenario's title card: *"We can cancel whatever you like, Mr. Secretary. But at this rate, when do we build carriers?"*

## 2. Strategy

### 2.1 Build sequencing — "the ladder never waits"

The General Board's escalation doctrine, fixed by 1918 and never made policy because it never needed to be: **lay the next generation down before the last has fired a full-caliber broadside.** Trials data would only slow the climb; the plant, not the fleet, sets the tempo.

**The interval is constant and so is the class size — a new generation every two years, of three hulls, without exception since 1923.** What varies is the bore, and it varies only twice in nine years. Money never binds: the economy is an order of magnitude larger than Japan's, and steel is not scarce either. **Armor plate and gun plant bind**, at roughly four times the 1922 industry — *and that is why the deception is public works.* Locks, docks, building ways, gun plants and armor mills are not treaty-limited and are not warships. The plant and the hulls are built together from the first year of the divergence, which is why the class size never has to fall.

**Each caliber gets two generations: one to prove the bore, one to build the ship the bore deserves.** The sixteen-inch was already in service and the founding rung is the answer to what the bombing trials did to a target ship, not to a rival's gun. The eighteen-inch is proved, mounted twelve times, then mounted fifteen. The twenty-one-and-a-half is proved, mounted nine times in triples, then twelve. ⚠ **The ways hold at three hulls because they were poured a generation ahead of the hulls — and that stops being true at the fifth, where the dock was specified before the ship and there are exactly two of them** (§4.3).

**And the gun plant sets the shape of the ladder as much as the ways set its width.** The plant is retooled ahead of the hull that will carry the result; when the retooling runs late, the doctrine forbids waiting, so the hull is drawn around the bore already in hand and grows in every other dimension instead. That is what the third generation is: the second generation's gun, a fifth turret group to make up the broadside, and twenty thousand more tons of ship.

**The cadence is shorter than the evaluation loop, and that is the scenario.** Nothing laid down in a generation has been to sea when the next keels go down. It is the American mirror of Japan's monoculture risk: Japan risks one flaw across many identical hulls; **America risks one flaw propagating up a ladder that cannot be halted, because two more generations are already on the ways — and because each bore is mounted twice, a flaw in a gun is a flaw in six ships rather than three.**

### 2.2 The Gun Club doctrine

The escalation generations share one design grammar — grouped triple turrets, 45° elevation, all-or-nothing armor deepened each rung, and the craftsmanship that makes them *work*: the founding generation commissioned clean and shot straight, which is the ladder's best argument for itself. Doctrine follows the guns. Fire beyond the horizon is solved by the world's best floatplane spotting corps and a radio-plot system built into every director; night gunnery is funded; damage control is a religion. What the grammar omits: the guns of each generation outrange its own eyes, the secondary batteries freeze at whatever the last refit funded, and **no hull in the fleet carries a provision for equipment that does not yet exist** — American refits are structural work at structural prices, every time. That single sentence is the whole American refit economy, and it is the exact inverse of the habit every rival is building into its own hulls.

### 2.3 Personnel — the cascade

The monsters eat the fleet from the inside, and the mechanism is not money. Ratings are not the problem — the yards' towns raise machinists faster than the Navy can enlist them. **Berths are the problem**, and the only pool deep enough is the battle line the ladder was built to replace: every monster commissioned strips crews from an older hull, reserve rows first and then the line itself. Officers and pilots are scarcer still — floatplane observation is the service's elite path, the carrier pipeline runs through one deck, and the staff college teaches gunnery convergence as the whole of the operational art. The arithmetic, and how far down it has already eaten, is §4.1.

### 2.4 Speed and range policy

⚠ **The battle line has moved at twenty-five knots since 1923 and every rung is drawn to hold it, exactly.** The plant grows to carry the displacement and never once to exceed the line; the per-class figures live in the catalog's machinery rows, and across nine years and a hundred and two thousand tons of growth the speed row is the only one that never moves. **Speed is not traded away so much as fixed and then defended**, on the doctrine that guns of this weight make retreat irrelevant. Endurance is the second: every generation is written to the same Pacific war-plan radius, made good at the fleet's common cruising speed with a fleet train sized to the transit — but each rung carries a thinner margin of bunker above that floor than the one before it, because the weight goes into shell and plate instead. The founding generation has the deepest bunkers the ladder will ever have, as a fraction of itself.

The constraint that actually binds is neither, and it is concrete. **The heavy generations do not fit the Panama locks** (§4.3), so strategic mobility is now a property of which ocean a hull was built in — and the frame owns the consequence, which is Cape Horn on the terms set out at `../hindsight-scenarios.md` §3.2.

## 4. National state

### 4.1 Personnel (the first governor)

**~135,000 uniformed, and ten commissioned monsters hold 35,350 of them — better than a quarter of the whole Navy afloat in fifteen ships.** Every monster is manned by cannibalism, and the cascade has already run further than the fleet list makes obvious. Commissioning the three United States hulls in 1933–35 took **~12,450 berths**, and the Navy found them by emptying the six reserve Standards and sending four older dreadnoughts to training and mothballs. **That pool is now spent.**

⚠ **And the arithmetic of what is left on the ways is the scenario, not a pressure inside it. The five hulls building need about 30,200 berths — near enough the entire complement of the pre-escalation battle line:** six South Dakotas, eight modernized Standards, six reserve Standards and four older dreadnoughts, to the man. **Finishing the ships already begun means paying off every capital ship the Navy owned before the ladder started.** The cascade is not fleet policy in all but name any more; it is the bill, and it falls due inside the scenario.

Aviation manning is the famine's ledger: **~260 carrier-qualified pilots exist**, the pipeline adds **~40 a year** through Ranger's one deck, and the floatplane corps (~700 pilots) is where careers are made. The gunnery ratings are the world's finest and know it.

### 4.2 The Ledger (the second governor)

The naval appropriation runs **~120,000 gold a year; the ladder and its plant take ~65%**, naval aviation under 4%. In a year like this one — six hulls building across three generations at once — the hull payments alone run to about **70,000 gold**, which is why every other line in the Navy is starved and why a single slipped delivery is a crisis rather than an inconvenience. The whole structure survives politically as Depression relief: eleven states hold monster work, **forty thousand yard jobs** ride on the two newest generations alone, and the committee chairmen are ladder men to a district. The trap is the recovery: every point of economic improvement converts the ladder from *relief* back into *spending*, contestable at every session. Fuel, steel and money are otherwise no constraint — this is the richest navy on Earth. What is scarce is permission, and after November, perhaps the President.

## 5. Rulings

⚠ **A ruling states what is true, and the file it lands in is the one that owns the fact.** The road file owns America's treaties, dates, the conference collapse and the ledger; this file owns the fleet, the rulings and the opening decisions.

### 5.1 The ladder is five generations of three hulls, on three calibers and two Marks

**Missouri 1923 · Constitution 1925 · United States 1927 · Tillman 1929 · Columbia 1932**, at **twenty-five knots throughout**. ⚠ **The interval is constant and so is the class size; what varies is the bore, and each bore is mounted in two generations** — one to prove it, one to build the ship it deserves. The cadence law itself is §2.1 above: a build is the scenario file's business, and `hindsight-scenarios.md` §3 holds the frame-level facts only.

### 5.2 What this file owns, and what it does not

America's 1916–35 timeline, her treaties, the collapse of the Second London Conference and the treaty ledger are `us-five-term-tillman-road.md`, which is the shape every nation uses. ⚠ **This file's §3 is The fleet and its §4 is National state**, matching the skeleton all four scenario files carry, so a citation of any scenario file's §N means the same thing in all four.

### 5.3 Two admiralty coefficients sit above the cap and are correct

⚠ **South Dakota C 242.6 and Ranger C 290.3 exceed the ×1.10 *Yamato* cap and are 11 to 16 percent UNDER their own same-class comparators.** The cap belongs to a Froude-number and hull-class domain neither ship is in, which conventions §12 names as the commonest false finding in this project. Every rung of the ladder itself sits at 224 to 228, inside the band and above raw *Yamato*.

### 5.4 The 1936 election: Tillman runs and loses, and Sherrill is inaugurated on 20 January 1937

`data/scenarios/in_good_faith_1936.json` models the ballot as an **interactive** event, and the stated default covers the fifteen configurations in which America is not the player. **The default is: he runs, he loses, and Wade Sherrill — nine years at the Treasury, whose position on the Navy is that the Navy is a great deal of money — takes the oath on 20 January 1937.**

**Why he loses, and it is a precondition and not a mood.** ⚠ **The 1936 ballot is the first one held after the lie became public.** The locks were inspected by the world's press at a ribbon-cutting in 1934 and the armor-plate arithmetic followed; the electorate that funded twelve monsters through the Depression on a relief argument gets its first chance to vote on them **knowing what they are** — and it votes eight weeks before the treaties expire, with two Columbias just laid down and their guns unproved. **He was born on 11 August 1847**, which made him sixty-nine when he was first elected in 1916 and makes him **eighty-nine at this ballot and still eighty-nine at the inauguration.** He turns ninety in the August after it. **A sixth term stopped being a political question some years ago.**

⚠ **THE OUTCOME IS THE DEFAULT AND NOT A RAIL.** A player who takes America plays the election; the ladder's political guarantor is what is actually at stake in §6.4, and a player who wins it keeps a cadence that has never once been questioned by an appropriation. **What canon fixes is what happens when nobody intervenes.**

⚠ **And note the clock this sits on. 31 DECEMBER 1936 IS THE TREATY EXPIRY, NOT THE END OF PLAY** — `in_good_faith_1936.json` runs to **31 December 1950**, with checkpoints at 1940, 1945 and 1950. **The ballot falls eight weeks before the treaties die and the inauguration three weeks after them**, so the succession lands in the gap between the old law ending and whatever replaces it. ⚠ **And its consequences are inside play for another fourteen years:** the ladder's cadence is a promise renewed every two years by one man, and §6.4 is the player finding out what it is worth without him.

### 5.5 The treaty ledger

The ledger at 1 January 1936 is `us-five-term-tillman-road.md` §4, because the road file owns the treaties it is drawn from.

### 5.6 The top caliber is 21.5 inches, in triples

`[us29gun]` is **21.5 in (546 mm) L/45 in a triple turret** — 2,300 kg shell, 800 m/s, 1.2 rpm a gun, 4,400 t a turret — and **the mounting never changes shape on the way up: three guns to a group at every bore.**

**The reason is a mounting study, not a doctrine.** Above twenty-two inches a triple's ring, hoists and flash arrangements outgrow any beam the Panama locks will ever pass, and a twin ladder pays a turret group's weight for every two barrels. At twenty-one and a half the triple works, so **the caliber came down in order that the battery could go up**: the Tillman carries **nine barrels in three triples** and the Columbia **twelve in four**.

**Both hulls are drawn to that battery.** Tillman: 112,000 t standard, 315 × 44.5 × 13.9 m, 170,000 shp, crew 5,200. Columbia: 142,000 t standard, 350 × 46.5 × 14.4 m, 198,000 shp, crew 6,600. ⚠ **The 44.5 m beam is held deliberately** — the lock miss stands at half a meter for the Tillmans and two and a half for the Columbias.

⚠ **The ledger follows the ladder: capital tonnage is ~2,221,000 t against 525,000 permitted — 423 percent** — 1,571,000 t on the register and 650,000 t building. **And the manning collision is tight without being closed:** ten commissioned monsters hold 35,350 berths and the five building need about 30,200, which is, to a rounding error, the whole pre-escalation battle line.

## 6. Opening decisions

1. **The cancellation question, asked properly.** The three Columbias are between three and five percent complete (§3.1). Canceled today they fund **twelve fleet carriers plus air groups** — but the 21.5-inch plant, the fifth-generation docks and the districts of §4.2 have no other customer, and the machine that protects the Navy runs on them. Cancel both, one, neither — or *suspend* ("slow build"), keeping the districts warm while the money leaks away a quarter at a time?
2. **The Republic conversion memo.** The Bureau of Aeronautics' unsigned study: a 112,000-ton hull at forty percent is the best carrier lower-hull in existence — 330 m of flight deck, a double hangar of some 22,000 m², a horizontal scheme that no bomb in the world will get through, and an air group no other navy could put on one deck. Converting her costs less than completing her as a battleship. It is also heresy, and the President reads the launching schedule personally.
3. **Panama, or the fleet splits.** The Tillmans miss the new locks by half a meter that concrete will not give back (§4.3), and the Columbias miss by two and a half. Fourth Locks: ~8 years and colossal cost, sized this time with a margin no gun can outgrow. Nicaragua: ~10 years and new treaty exposure. Or accept what is already true: an Atlantic monster fleet, a Pacific everything-else, and a war plan that assumes the two never need to swap. **The allocation is permanent, it is made this year, and the movable ships are the oldest and weakest.**
4. **November.** The fifth term runs out in January and the President intends to run for a sixth (`us-five-term-tillman-road.md` §1) — which puts the succession question *inside* the scenario's twelve months instead of safely beyond them. Back him and the ladder continues on its own momentum, with the Navy Department's institutional weight visibly behind an incumbent; spend twenty years of accumulated presidential capital on a managed transition instead, knowing the Air Lobby's candidate calls the ladder itself the enemy. Either way the ballot, the expiry and the inauguration fall inside eleven weeks of each other, in that order.
5. **The screen.** The monsters steam behind flush-deckers designed in 1917 — eight Farraguts are the whole modern screen, and not one of them carries a sonar set. One year of Tillman money buys thirty modern destroyers. The Gun Club calls escorts "the help." The player has read about what happens to unscreened capital ships in narrow waters.
6. **Crews.** Finish the five hulls on the ways on schedule and pay off the pre-escalation battle line entire (§4.1), or slip the schedule and keep hulls-in-being? ⚠ **The cascade is arithmetic and it no longer has any slack in it: the berths the five need and the berths the old fleet holds are the same number.** Only the timing is a choice.
7. **The Japan question.** Tokyo's decks — four, or eleven if she is the other monster on this board, and a filed conclusion: the answer to a gun that cannot be outweighed is something that does not weigh anything. Carrier aviation, an aerial torpedo, land-based naval bombers, night work against anchored capital ships — **none of it funded this morning, all of it written.** Answer in kind (carriers from a cold start, into the famine), answer in kilotons (radar, dual-purpose batteries, and the spotting corps turned into an air-defense net), or answer in geography (the distant-blockade Orange revision — the monsters as the wall the swarm must eventually come to)? **The choice is cheap today and dear in five years, and the intelligence is good enough that nobody gets to be surprised.**

## 7. Standing pressures (emergent, always on)

Every monster is a prototype: the standard teething system rolls honest dice against fifteen-gun broadsides, first-of-class plants at a scale nothing afloat has run, and turret structures carrying roller-path loads nothing has ever carried — and each rung of the ladder resets the risk at larger scale, ⚠ **twice over, because each bore is mounted in two generations and a flaw in a gun is a flaw in six ships.** The cascade converts the old fleet into crew stock one commissioning at a time, and has run out of ships nobody was using. The aviation hole deepens by a full year, every year — pipelines cannot sprint, and a pipeline running through a single deck is a pipeline in name only. The locks are concrete: every strategic argument now happens on one side of them or the other. The elections tick, and this year the ballot is inside the twelve months and the inauguration three weeks past them. Over all of it runs a date the President did not choose, and it falls between them: **the treaties expire, and every rival's program stops being a number somebody else agreed to** (`us-five-term-tillman-road.md` §1). And the ladder's own logic never sleeps: each generation exists to obsolete the last, so *stopping anywhere* is an admission that the whole climb had a top. And one line item has appeared in every Bureau of Ordnance estimate since 1931 and been struck every year: full-charge proof firings of the fleet's one torpedo exploder.

## 8. What the rivals will plan from 1 January 1936

**Nothing in this section has been built.** Every rival navy is its **plain historical self on 1 January 1936** — historical hulls, historical estimates, historical programs (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §1.3). The deception held; nobody funded an answer to a fleet nobody was certain existed, and the years in which the leaks did land were the years nobody had the money.

**What the rivals have instead is paper, and a great deal of it.** Thinking is free and staff colleges are cheap: the studies were run, the war games played, the plans revised, the estimates costed — and then filed, because the money was not there. **The reaction is real, it is written, and it is unfunded.** On the first day of play it comes out of the cabinet, and the estimates for the coming fiscal year are drafted in the winter of 1936–37 by governments that have just watched a conference collapse.

**Where a rival is itself toggled on as a divergence, this section does not describe it** — it is running its own strategy, and none of the below applies.

**Japan.** Never answered the ladder at all — she was already off the road on her own account, and the arithmetic ran the same way whether Washington built monsters or not. Four flight decks — Hōshō, Akagi, Kaga, Ryūjō — a fifth fitting out, and the conclusion reached and filed is that a gun which cannot be outweighed must be attacked by something that does not weigh anything: carrier aviation, an aerial torpedo good enough to matter, land-based long-range naval bombers, and night torpedo work drilled against anchored capital ships. **None of it is funded on 1 January 1936 and all of it is written.** The staff college's ladder file is titled, in translation, *"The Answer Is Not a Larger Nail."*

**Britain.** Intends to keep its money. The King George V line proceeds on treaty-sized hulls with a serious Fleet Air Arm beside it, and every monster study the Admiralty has run ends in the same minute — *the docks to fight it cost more than the fleet to sink it.* London's real fear is the precedent and the date: **if displacement is uncapped for Washington it is uncapped for everyone, and at the end of this year it is uncapped by operation of law.**

**France and Italy.** Treat the ladder as an American eccentricity, will go on pricing their programs against each other, and will sell nothing but opinions.

**Germany.** Will cite the expiring Washington and London treaties as vindication for rebuilding, and will keep its own designs at European scale. Krupp's export office intends to bid quietly on 21.5-inch forging work — an offer the Bureau will decline, and file.

**Soviet Union.** The only government that admires the ladder without irony. The design bureaus will sketch answers they cannot build.

**Everyone's war college** has run the same wargame and filed the same result under *"eventually, the airplane."* The only dissent is about the date — and every date on file is later than the Japanese one.

## 9. Data & schema implications

1. **Equipment, designations, provisions:** per `docs/equipment-conventions.md`; US catalog SKUs in `data/equipment/`. The United States has **no engine-cartridge SKUs**, so its machinery rows take the non-modular form — plant · shp · speed · range @ cruise — and range appears there once and nowhere else.
2. **Budget & politics:** Congressional appropriation gates on every program change (cancel, suspend, convert, new line); district-employment pressure state feeding the standard politics system; the election events of §5.4 with Gun Club / Air Lobby faction outcomes; recovery index decays the ladder's relief justification each year.
3. **Prototype & teething risk:** standard system, scaled per rung — first-of-generation flags on the third, fourth and fifth generations' plants and turrets, and on the [us29gun] proof line, modeled as a live development risk.
4. **Infrastructure registry:** locks with usable beams and over-sill depths (§4.3), transit constraints binding on hull beam *and draft*; fifth-generation dock completion dates; Fourth Locks and Nicaragua as standard infrastructure projects with cost and duration from §6.3.
5. **Conversion-in-build:** the Republic memo (§6.2) requires it; it is core (Kaga needed it in 1923). Carrier conversion of a battleship hull part-built uses standard yard mechanics, no special rule.
6. **Crew pools & pipelines:** rating classes with the reserve and modernized Standards as first-class crew pools drawn down in that order; carrier-pilot pipeline throughput scaling with decks rather than budget alone; floatplane corps as a separate rating line.
7. **Aviation industrial base:** engine line warm (commercial market), airframe lines cold, carrier curve position first-of-class — the famine is data, not a rule.
8. **No reactive-program entries.** Nothing in this pack models another nation's response to the ladder; every non-divergent power loads from the plain historical 1 January 1936 data, and Japan's own divergence, when toggled on, loads from her own pack. **Nothing merges at load time** (`../hindsight-scenarios.md` §5.2).
9. **National pack: DELIVERED** — `data/ships/hindsight/us_five_term_tillman_pack.json` + `data/equipment/us_five_term_tillman.json`. Base 1922 data untouched, referenced and never modified. **The pack authors every class in the LITERAL style** (`propulsion` with the cruising speed stated directly in the range clause), because this navy has no engine-cartridge SKUs, and it declares every hull's provisions as *absent* — which is how the schema records a fleet that provisions nothing and pays structural prices forever.
