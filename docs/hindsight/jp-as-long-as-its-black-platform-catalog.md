## Calibration position (conventions §12)

Japan bought a **system** rather than a set of weapons, and almost everything that runs deep in this navy is architecture or process. The few performance figures that lead are named below beside the mechanism that produces them.

| Band | Lines |
|---|---|
| **Interest — up to +5** | **The standardization system** — one hull per role, one gun, one torpedo, one engine cartridge, block construction and the hydroformed panel line. **The provision habit**, which wires a hull for equipment nobody has yet. **The dual-purpose director family**, against the American Mark 33 of 1934, the first anywhere. **The overhaul-life mechanism** — one design per role, one plant, 30 years, no variants and no mid-life gamble, every engine understressed for its rating. **Modern technique** — the hydroformed panel line and what it does to structure — at 5 percent of empty weight on every airplane in this navy, the empty weights themselves derived against a **Mitsubishi A5M4 at 1,216 kg, an A6M2 Model 21 at 1,680, a Mitsubishi Ki-46-II at 3,263 and a MiG-15bis at 3,681** and standing at **+17, +5, +21 and +1 percent** on them. |

## 1. Ships

### 1.1 Unryū class — Type 32 fleet carrier (1932)

Every elevator on this ship works both hangar decks, and all of them sit at the deck edge, which is the cleverest single decision in her design. The drawings were frozen years before any of her steel was cut, and the yards that eventually cut it were built as block halls around that one drawing. She commissioned with an armored plot room and a fighter-direction radio net wired and empty, waiting on a radar set nobody on earth could yet build. Her protection amounts to cofferdammed aviation fuel, inert gas and split electrical power, and there is no belt and no armored deck anywhere on her. For years she embarked one light general-purpose type and nothing else, which left her deck crews superb at moving an aircraft that could not fight. Judged as a hull she is the best thing this navy has ever drawn, and she spent her early years as a beautifully organized empty box.

### 1.2 Maya class — Type 29 cruiser (1929)

Her published weight was a fiction, and because every hull of the class is the same hull it was the same fiction repeated down the whole line. The main battery is a light dual-purpose gun in power-worked turrets, and there is more of it aboard than a hull this size usually carries. She went to sea with her automatic-cannon battery fitted and working rather than wired for a later refit, which no other navy's cruiser could claim in her year. As launched she was slow, badly slow for a cruiser, and the cure was a change of engine cartridges rather than a rebuild. Her armor is a box over the machinery and the magazines and nothing else, and she was delivered with optical rangefinders above an empty director seat. Taken one at a time she is unremarkable, and taken as a drawing nobody is permitted to alter she is the most successful hull in the fleet.

### 1.3 Kaze class — Type 32 destroyer (1932)

Drawn years before her first keel and then left on the shelf deliberately, she waited for a mature engine cartridge and a director that worked. The result is a ship with no teething history at all, because nothing aboard her was new when the first one commissioned. She carries the standard main turret, centerline launchers for the fleet torpedo, sonar, depth charges and mine rails, which is a heavy outfit for the type. She is also built to run faster than the body she screens, and she does it on the same cartridge everything else in the fleet burns. Her complement is lean even by the standards of a navy that prides itself on lean complements, and lean crews are wonderful until something floods. By weight and battery she is a small light cruiser wearing a destroyer's label, and she is the most convincing fighting ship the standardization produced.

### 1.4 I-series — Type 33 submarine (1933)

No gun anywhere on her, and that absence is the design in one word. Her diesels never touch a shaft; they make current, and motors do all the driving on the surface and beneath it. The cells were multiplied and the hull faired for the water she fights in rather than the water she crosses, so she runs quicker submerged than surfaced. That is a genuinely radical boat, and it is undercut by the one figure nobody was willing to move, because she does not go deep. On the surface she is sluggish for an ocean boat, which matters when a patrol line has to be reached before anybody notices the transit. She is the boldest hull in this plan, and what she does deep and quiet has never appeared in print anywhere.

### 1.5 Shima class — Type 32 escort (1932)

She was funded under the fisheries-protection line, and the only untrue part of that description was the fish. The cover let a sonar school put to sea in peacetime with nobody asking a question, and the school is the reason the hull exists. She mounts a full-size main turret forward, which is a great deal of gun for a ship this small, and optical sights are all she has to lay it. Her depth-charge outfit is deep, her sonar is the only set this navy makes, and against a submarine she is honest and unglamorous work. A foundation for a centerline torpedo launcher sits plated and empty under her deck, waiting for the day somebody stops pretending about her. She is the cheapest hull anybody here knows how to repeat, and no foreign service would accept her as a warship on any terms.

### 1.6 Standard Maru — Type 23 merchant & escort carrier (1923)

A freighter, and probably the most valuable drawing this navy ever produced. One midbody takes dry cargo, oil or troops, and the choice is made at a pier instead of in a design office. Flight-deck foundations were worked into the hull years before the conversion kit existed and before there was anything worth flying off the result. There is a barbette ring under plating on her poop with no gun issued against it, and wired positions for cannon that were never fitted. Her engine bays lend cartridges to warships at sea, and the conversion time claimed for her has never been checked by anybody outside. As a carrier she is poor, with a short deck, piston aircraft only, and not a gun aboard her on the day she sails.

## 2. Aircraft

### 2.1 Hibari — Type 33 light universal multirole (1933)

There is nothing clever about this machine except how long it stays in the air. The wing is large and long, and it folds tighter than it ought to because every drop of fuel rides in the fuselage instead. She has no protection of any kind, no high-lift device and a propeller that cannot change pitch, which is a trainer's specification. One crutch under her belly takes a heavy bomb, a stick of depth charges or a mine, and a twin cannon in the nose is her whole fighting armament. For years she was the only type on any flight deck in this navy, which says a great deal more about the plan than about her. As a scout, a trainer and a patrol machine she is entirely adequate, and against anything with a fighter's performance she is a casualty.

### 2.2 Raiden — Type 39 fighter (1939)

She carries nothing she cannot fight with: no rack, no crutch, no plumbing and no drop tank anywhere on the airframe. The fuel that would have hung outside rides inside instead, which gives a single-seat fighter an absurd amount of time on station. Her span was set by the hangar fold rather than by aerodynamics, so area within that span became the only maneuver lever the designers had left. She is not fast for her year, and anybody expecting the sealed gaps and flush riveting to have produced something startling will be disappointed. What she does carry is armor glass, a plated seat, self-sealing tanks and a cartridge-rail ejection seat, which is more care for a pilot than most air arms showed. A twin cannon with a short burst of ammunition behind it is thin armament, and she is still the right fighter for the navy that built her.

### 2.3 Tenzan — Type 39 strike (1939)

The best aircraft this navy owns is also the worst one to leave in a hurry. Every seat fires on one rail, all together, onto a single linked parachute, and nobody has watched that arrangement used in anger. Her whole load rides in a conformal belly recess, so a torpedo, bombs, a mine or ferry tanks all leave the airframe clean. Radar came with her as delivered equipment rather than as a modification, which was unheard of on a carrier strike machine of her generation. Empty she becomes a heavy fighter, and on her own radar a night fighter, deadly against bombers and helpless against a single-seater in daylight. She cannot escort anything, including herself, so every strike she flies has to be covered by somebody else.

### 2.4 Shinden — Type 44 jet universal multirole (1944)

Nineteen forty-four brought this navy a jet, and the astonishing part is that it plugs straight into interfaces drawn a generation earlier. The recess in her belly is still shaped around a torpedo weight frozen long before anybody here had heard of a turbojet. The same cannon, the same beacon set, the same stores and the same catapult rating all carry across to her unchanged. She drinks, and a machine with this little endurance makes an odd universal type for a navy that thinks in oceans. Her wing loading is savage and she crosses the ramp faster than anything with a propeller ever asked a deck to accept. She folds at the tips alone, so an airframe a generation ahead of everything around it has to live in a hangar drawn for propellers.
