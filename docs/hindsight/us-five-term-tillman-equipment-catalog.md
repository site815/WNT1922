## 3. Weapons

### 3.1 Super-heavy gun line — Mark 1 (1925)

One gun plant made every bore on the escalation ladder, and it was retooled upward twice rather than rebuilt for each step. The founding weapon is a wartime pattern already in service when the climb began, and it sits outside the Mark sequence entirely. The mounting is a triple at every bore with the guns individually sleeved, because a plant that scales one architecture never has to tool a second. Elevation and maximum range are common to the whole family, so a turret crew and a plotting room move up a rung without relearning the drill. Rate of fire falls as the bore climbs, and the Bureau of Ordnance has never treated that as a fault. The top mounting has no equivalent anywhere at any date, and what nobody outside the country can establish is how deep the stock of forgings behind it runs.

| Turret | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Triple mounts, individually sleeved guns · 45° elevation · 40 km at maximum elevation · shellroom-to-gun power handling · six-bag charges |
| *(1918 pattern — outside the Mark sequence)* | 16 in (406 mm) L/50 · 952 kg armor-piercing shell · 853 m/s · 1.25 rounds per minute (rpm) per gun · triple turret 1,750 t |
| [us25gun] | 18 in (457 mm) L/48 · 1,315 kg armor-piercing shell · 823 m/s · 1.5 rpm/gun · triple turret 2,500 t |
| [us29gun] | 21.5 in (546 mm) L/45 · 2,300 kg armor-piercing shell · 800 m/s · 1.2 rpm/gun · triple turret 4,400 t |

⚠ **The eighteen-inch is the real American article at its own decade's figures and is exact par; the twenty-one-and-a-half-inch has no equivalent at any date and is plant architecture under §12's premise clause, its shell the cube-law scaling of the two real bores below it.** *The Bureau of Ordnance writes 21.5 in/45 Mark 2 and has issued one minute on the subject, declining to record the wardroom's arithmetic about what a broadside weighs.*

### 3.2 Secondary battery line — Mark 1 (1925)

Rearming a hull up this line is the one refit an American battleship gets without cutting the ship open. Shellrooms, hoists and the fuze well are common to every mounting in the family, so a newer gun drops onto an older ship's ring. The earliest article is an open high-angle single worked by hand, and for a decade it was the fleet's whole answer to an attack from the air. After it comes a base-ring mounting, single or twin, that fights ships and aircraft with the same barrels, and it is as good as any afloat. The last of them is enclosed and longer in the barrel, and its reach is short for a gun of that length. Nothing else aboard an American hull is this easy to improve, and the reason is one caliber chosen early and never questioned.

| Mount | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | 127 mm caliber · semi-fixed ammunition · common shellrooms, hoists and fuze well across all mounts |
| [us25sec] | L/25 high-angle single, open mounting · 24.4 kg shell · 657 m/s · 13.3 km / 8,400 m ceiling · 14 rpm/gun · 20 t |
| [us34sec] | L/38 dual-purpose, single or twin base-ring mounting · 25 kg shell · 790 m/s · 15.9 km / 11,400 m ceiling · 18 rpm/gun · single 29 t / twin 43 t |
| [us45sec] | L/54 dual-purpose single, enclosed base-ring mounting · 31.8 kg shell · 808 m/s · 16.6 km / 13,700 m ceiling · 16 rpm/gun · 45 t |

### 3.3 Quad anti-aircraft cannon — Mark 1 (1936)

How many of these mountings are actually at sea is not something an outsider can count. Nothing in this fleet is drawn with a seat for them, so each one goes aboard by cutting steel, paid out of whatever appropriation was current. The gun itself is sound, with water-cooled barrels on a quadruple mounting and high-explosive tracer as the only nature carried. Clips go in by hand, so how long a mounting keeps firing is a property of its loaders rather than of a magazine. The later weapon is a foreign design built under license, power-trained and director-capable, and it is a serious gun where the first was a stopgap. Close-range air defense in this navy is an intention with a paper strength, and the mountings to make it real arrive one appropriation at a time.

| Cannon | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Quad mounting · water-cooled barrels · clip-fed by hand · high-explosive tracer, one round type |
| [us36can] | 28 × 199 mm · 0.42 kg shell · 150 rpm/barrel · 8-round clips · quad mount 6.4 t |
| [us41can] | 40 × 311 mm, license-built · 0.9 kg shell · 160 rpm/barrel · 4-round clips · power-trained, director-capable · quad mount 11 t |

### 3.4 Quad torpedo mount — Mark 1 (1934)

No reload stowage was drawn into this mount, and none has ever been fitted to a ship that carries it. What sits in the tubes is what a flotilla attack is worth, and afterwards the boats are gun platforms and nothing more. The mount trains under power right round on a centerline seat and fires on a gyro angle passed from the director. That is competent modern practice and matches anything afloat. It serves only where it was built in, and no ship has ever received one as an addition. One throw is a thin proposition against a battle line, and nothing in the fleet's published practice suggests a second was ever planned for.

| Mount | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us34lau] | Quad mount · 18 t · powered train, 360° on centerline mountings · fired on gyro angle from the director |

## 4. Sub-equipment

### 4.1 Main-battery director — Mark 1 (1927)

The director is this navy's actual weapon and the guns are its output. Fire beyond the horizon is the declared working condition, which means an airplane over the target feeds corrections into the plot as a matter of routine. A rangekeeper works in the plotting room behind armor, and coincidence rangefinders sit in the director top and in the turret roofs. The glass is ordinary for its year, and that is a surprise in a fleet whose whole doctrine rests on seeing further than anybody else. Its optics come from two naval shops that supply nothing else, so a damaged instrument is replaced from a crate rather than returned to a maker. The later Mark feeds radar range and bearing into the plot and puts turret train under remote power control, which is what makes the extreme ranges honest.

| Director | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us27dir] | Rangekeeper computer (plotting room) · stable vertical element · director top with 4.6 m coincidence rangefinder · coincidence elements to 12.5 m base for turret mountings · 9 t head · aircraft-spot correction input at the plot |
| [us40dir] | Radar range and bearing feed from [us39rad] into the plot · remote power control of turret train |

### 4.2 Dual-purpose director — Mark 3 (1934)

Tachymetric prediction exists in this fleet in one place, and this family is it. The earliest of them carries its computer up in the head with the rangefinder, which keeps it light and puts delicate gear out in the spray. The next generation moves the computer below decks, adds a stable element and a larger head, and grows heavy enough that only a big ship will carry it. This is the reason a dual-purpose battery here amounts to more than a gun that happens to elevate. The last Mark ties the whole system into radar and works with a proximity-fuzed round, which turns a good air-defense outfit into a lethal one. The equipment is ahead of the fleet using it, since a director can only aim the mountings a ship was funded to carry.

| Director | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us34dir] | Tachymetric dual-purpose computer in the director head (topside) · 3.5 m rangefinder · 5 t |
| [us36dir] | Computer below decks · stable element · 4.5 m rangefinder head · 16 t |
| [us44dir] | Radar ranging and blind-fire integration with [us43rad] · proximity-fuze engagement with [us43shl] |

### 4.3 Ship radar — Mark 1 (1939)

Every set in this navy goes into a hull that was never drawn to take one, which makes each fitting a structural job. No mast, no wiring run and no power margin was ever reserved anywhere in the fleet for gear of this kind. The requirement was written by people trying to lay a gun rather than find a formation, and the set reflects that from end to end. The first generation ranges on capital targets far past anything a masthead can see, and it finds formations at a useful distance. The centimetric set that follows ranges blind, spots the fall of shot and puts a plan position display in front of an operator. That last capability is the one that matters, because a battery which outshoots its own glass has been waiting a long while for something to see with.

| Ship radar | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us39rad] | Air search 110 km (formations) · surface 25 km · ranging on capital targets to 30 km |
| [us43rad] | Centimetric: blind-fire ranging ±35 m · fall-of-shot spotting to 40 km · plan position indicator (PPI) display |

### 4.4 Sonar — Mark 1 (1934)

Budgets decided which ships got a set, and hulls that badly needed one went without. The newest destroyers in the fleet reached the water with no listening gear aboard at all, and that single fact is the American position on submarines entire. The set itself is an ordinary active echo-ranging outfit with a chemical range recorder, neither better nor worse than what other navies use. Range is short, and conditions rule it so completely that the school's tables matter more than the transducer does. The later generation holds contact right in to the drop and feeds a pattern to the stern racks, which is a real improvement on guesswork. The equipment is unremarkable and the trouble lies elsewhere, in a service that has never treated the submarine as a serious problem.

| Sonar | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us34snr] | Active 24 kHz: 1,400 m reliable / 2,700 m good conditions · chemical range recorder |
| [us42snr] | Maintained-contact recorder · stern-rack pattern fire-control output |

### 4.5 Utility aero engine — Mark 1 (1929)

Nothing about this engine was asked for by a navy. It was developed for the civil market and adopted because it was what the industry was already building in quantity. A single-row radial with no blower, it makes its rated power down low and loses it steadily on the way up. Spares come off a commercial stores line, and the artificer schools teach it because every school in the country teaches it. No successor was ever specified, and no second generation of the family exists anywhere an outsider can see. For a floatplane thrown off a catapult to sit above the fall of shot, that is adequate, and adequate is the whole ambition here.

| Aero engine | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us29aeg] | 9-cyl radial · 30 L · 450 kg · 575 hp takeoff / 500 at 1,500 m · time between overhauls (TBO) 400 h |

### 4.6 Combat aero engine — Mark 2 (1933)

Both engines in this family bolt to the same mounts, and that is the most useful thing about the later one. The first is a two-row radial with a single-speed blower, modest in displacement, and it wants overhaul often enough to be a nuisance. Its successor is larger, heavier, two-speed supercharged and makes far more power high up, and it runs much longer between strips. That successor came off a commercial transport line rather than out of a naval program, and there is the whole of this fleet's aero-engine policy. An airframe designed around the first can take the second without being redrawn, which is more than most engine changes anywhere in the world allow. The engines are good, and there is nothing worth putting them in.

|---|---|

| [us38aeg] | 30 L two-row from the commercial transport line · 650 kg · 1,200 hp takeoff / 1,000 at 4,400 m · two-speed supercharger · same mounts · TBO 500 h |

### 4.8 Aircraft catapult and crane — Mark 1 (1923)

A powder charge throws the airplane off, which is the same chemistry that throws the shell. The track sits on a training turntable, the recovery crane stands on the quarterdeck, and the gunnery department works both. That last fact explains the fitting better than any of its dimensions do. It is the oldest article the fleet still buys and nobody has redrawn it, because the spotting airplane it throws has never grown heavier. Launching is brisk and recovery is the hard half, since a ship must make a slick and slow while a crane picks an airplane out of the water. For a navy whose guns shoot beyond their own glass, this unglamorous gear sits closer to fire control than to aviation.

| Fitting | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | Powder-charge track catapult on a training turntable · quarterdeck recovery crane · worked by the gunnery department |
| [us23cat] | **Mark 1** — 2.8 t to 100 km/h in 18 m · 1 launch every 40 s · crane 2.8 t at 11 m outreach · catapult 21 t |

### 5.2 Float and wheel exchange set — Mark 1 (1936)

This is a stores convention before it is a piece of equipment. Floats and a wheel undercarriage are ordered, stored and accounted as one item, so a squadron keeps a single line in its books instead of two. The exchange is a yard, tender or beach job measured in days rather than hours, and it needs no facility the fleet does not already own. Floats are the operational fit, and every printed figure for the airplane already carries their penalty. On wheels the same airframe is faster at every height, climbs better and reaches further, which is a great deal to hand back from a change of undercarriage. A navy that catapults its scouts off battleships will fly the float fit almost always, so the better half of the set mostly sits in a shed.

| Kit | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us36flt] | Twin-float and wheel undercarriages as one exchange set · yard, tender or beach evolution, about three days · floats +260 kg over the wheel fit · on wheels: +25 km/h at every band, +1.0 m/s climb, +10% combat radius |

## 6. Ordnance & drop tanks

### 6.1 Steam torpedo — Mark 1 (1931)

A magnetic influence exploder is this weapon's whole claim on the world. Fired under a keel it is meant to do what no contact hit can, and the fleet's torpedo doctrine is written on the assumption that it works. It has never been fired at full charge, and the line asking for proof shots is struck from the ordnance estimates in the year it appears, every year. The body underneath is an honest steam wet-heater with a good warhead, and the running depth is put on at the tube. Later generations revise the exploder and the depth-keeping and finally offer a wakeless electric body, and an article does not get revised in both places for nothing. Every part of this weapon is competent except the part everything depends on, and nobody has yet spent the money to find out.

| Torpedo | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | 53 cm × 6.3 m · 1,570 kg · 300 kg warhead · steam wet-heater · dual exploder: magnetic influence + contact · running depth set at the tube · full-charge proof firings: none conducted |
| [us31tor] | Fast 46 kt / 4,100 m · slow 31.5 kt / 8,200 m |
| [us42tor] | Revised exploder and depth-keeping |
| [us44tor] | Electric wakeless body · single setting 29 kt / 3,700 m |

### 6.2 Depth charge — Mark 1 (1924)

There is one way to deliver this weapon, and it is to roll it off the stern. That means driving over the contact and letting the drums go, with nothing in service that reaches ahead of the bow. The pattern is settled by the ship's speed and the fuze settings, and both are decided before the run begins. The later drum is heavier, carries more explosive and reaches a depth the first one could not touch, which is the family's only real development. Its effect depends on knowing where the submarine is, and knowing is the harder half of the problem by a wide margin. Against a boat that dives deep and runs quiet this is last-war equipment, and the fleet has ordered nothing to replace it.

| Depth charge | Specification |
|---|---|
| [us24dpc] | 140 kg drum · 80 kg burster · hydrostatic fuze 30–90 m |
| [us39dpc] | 260 kg drum · 150 kg burster · hydrostatic fuze to 150 m |

### 6.3 Five-inch shell — Mark 1 (1925)

The fuze well in this round was cut with a cavity nobody had a fuze to fill. Years later a proximity fuze dropped straight into it, with no change to the projectile, the well, the setter or the hoists. In a navy that works nothing into a ship for equipment it does not yet own, that piece of foresight stands alone. The family runs common, anti-aircraft and illuminating natures on semi-fixed ammunition, and the mechanical time fuze is set at the mounting. Each projectile is drawn to the bore rather than to the mounting, so every gun in the line takes its own weight of shell down a common hoist. It is the least glamorous article in the whole inventory and among the two or three that are genuinely first-rate.

| Shell | Specification |
|---|---|
| Common | 127 mm semi-fixed ammunition · common, anti-aircraft and illuminating natures · universal fuze well with a proximity-ready cavity |
| [us25shl] | Mechanical time fuze · point-detonating and non-delay settings · fuze setter at the mount |
| [us43shl] | Proximity (variable-time) fuze in the same cavity, same ballistics |

### 6.4 Drop tank — Mark 1 (1937)

Hanging this tank means hanging no bomb, because both ride the same centerline crutch. The requirement for it came out of the gunnery branch and not out of aviation at all. A battery that outranges its own rangefinder needs an observer who can stay up, and this is the article that keeps him there. It is pressed steel, expendable and never retained for landing, so a squadron writes one off with every flight that carries it. Only the scout-bomber can take it, since the fighter has light wing racks and no centerline point whatever. Nobody in the requirement chain ever asked what a fighter's radius was for, which is a remarkable thing to be able to say about a navy this rich.

|---|---|
